## The World Dungeon's Basic Rules

Earth's surviving population is offered a choice: stay on the surface with no further resources,
or enter the 18-level World Dungeon to "reclaim" lost matter. Level 1 opens with 150,000
entrances; each subsequent level's entrance count halves (rounding up), down to just two
entrances (and one exit) on Level 18. Each level has a countdown timer — 5 days for Level 1,
increasing afterward based on factors like ratings and surviving crawler count — after which the
level "collapses" and anyone still on it forfeits everything, crawler included. There is no
respawn: "Dead is dead." Descending a staircase fully restores a crawler's health.

Floors 1–3 are seeded with **Tutorial Guilds**, run by non-combatant NPCs like
[Mordecai](../characters/mordecai.md), who explain the system to new crawlers and unlock their
inventory access. Starting on Floor 10, crawlers are offered options to exit the dungeon
entirely — better options the deeper they've gone. Floor 4 onward introduces random level
themes, guarded/quest-gated entrances, and much smaller playable areas than the near-planet-sized
first three floors.

## Skills

Every real-world skill and habit a crawler has ever practiced is tracked, filterable by level —
Carl's initial unfiltered list included things like *Breathing* and *Operating a Sony Brand
RMVLZ620 Universal Remote Control*. Combat- and dungeon-relevant skills (Unarmed Combat,
Pugilism, etc.) level up with use and stack multiplicatively with gear bonuses.

## Dimensional Inventory

This season introduced an upgrade over the prior slot-based system: unlimited storage, restricted
only by whether the crawler can physically lift an item off the ground unassisted for about four
seconds. Storage is time-locked (food doesn't spoil) and cannot hold living creatures. A
10-slot **hotlist** allows one-click use of spells, potions, and items without opening the full
inventory menu.

## Fog of War

The parts of the minimap a crawler hasn't personally explored yet render without detail, even
when technically within minimap range — this applies to ordinary terrain, but not to Safe Rooms
or stairwells, which always reveal themselves once in range regardless of exploration status.
See [Locations & Places to Go](locations.md) for Safe Zones, Tutorial/Training Guilds, and
Reward Rooms in full detail.

## Mob Tagging

Mobs and other crawlers show as colored dots on the minimap: green is the player, blue is other
crawlers, white is non-hostile/NPC, red is hostile. The same white/red coding is used for
individual creatures' name tags (e.g. a "red-tagged" pet is inherently dangerous/aggressive; a
"white-tagged" one is docile).

## Tattoos as Faction Markers

Certain loot boxes brand a permanent tattoo directly onto a crawler rather than adding an item to
inventory (e.g. the **Goblin Pass**, burned onto Carl's forearm). These tattoos function as
faction passes — granting safe passage through a faction's territory, sometimes at a cost (the
Goblin Pass increases damage taken from fairy-class enemies by 20%) — and can't be hidden without
a specific coverup item.

## Achievements

Nearly every notable in-game action triggers a system achievement: a short, often crude comedic
message, sometimes an in-voice bit (a caveman voice, an Attenborough impression, etc.), that
grants a named reward. Achievements are the dungeon's primary loot-box distribution mechanism.

## Wild Magic Bursts (Book 2)

A distinct hazard from ordinary magical explosions: a "wild magic" event unfolds as four
escalating precursor bursts roughly five minutes apart, each with a different, unpredictable
effect (the one witnessed strips the magical properties from all currently-equipped gear) before
a final, far more destructive detonation. Removing magical items and placing them safely in
inventory between bursts is the only real defense — items left equipped can activate on their
own, permanently break, or explode.

## Personal Spaces (Book 3)

A crawler's personal space can be upgraded to allow whitelisting other parties in — Katia does
this for Brynhild's Daughters, letting thirty visitors into the shared space at once. When two
separate parties who aren't grouped together need to interact (as with Hekla's team), the system
generates a **temporary, situationally-created space**: a new vestibule door appears next to the
personal space's own entrance, leading to the other party's space, without merging the two spaces
or requiring either side to actually party up.

## The Bubbles (Book 4)

Floor 5's structure: 1,172 sealed spherical "bubbles" sitting in sunken pockets of a dark
in-between space called the **Lacuna**, each holding roughly 150 crawlers. Every bubble is split
into four **quadrants** — Land, Sea (Water), Air, and Subterranean — each with its own castle and
throne room, separated from each other by an invisible barrier that blocks spells, thrown objects
under a caster's control, and physical crossing alike (a rolled ball can pass through; a held
stick cannot). All four quadrants' castles must be taken before a bubble's stairwell opens, and
every bubble typically has one "easy" environmental shortcut per quadrant that bypasses its
throne-room boss entirely — see [Denise the Feral Goose
Mother](../bosses/denise-the-feral-goose-mother.md), [Mrs. Ghazi](../bosses/mrs-ghazi.md), and
[Quetzalcoatlus](../bosses/quetzalcoatlus.md) for the three found in bubble #543.

## The Bounty-Doubling Leaderboard (Book 4)

Starting on Floor 5, any crawler who survives a floor while already ranked in the top ten receives
a permanent **"(x2)"** bounty multiplier on all future leaderboard listings, via an achievement
called "The Quarry Sees Another Spring." This stacks with each qualifying floor survived.

## Glass Cannon Class (Book 4)

A specialization choice for Donut's Former Child Actor class: forbids further Constitution
training entirely, but grants a flat +15 base Constitution, boosts spell training speed, and
lowers spell mana costs — plus scales the level and duration cap on resurrection-type spells like
*Second Chance* well beyond its normal limits.

## Deity Worship, Feral Gods, and the Nothing (Book 4)

Building on the involuntary-summoning mechanics established with Grull (see
[War Gauntlet of the Exalted Grull](../items/war-gauntlet-of-the-exalted-grull.md)), Book 4
introduces the wider theology behind the dungeon's deities. **The Nothing** is a banishment
dimension the true pantheon uses to imprison gods, demons, and lesser deities judged too dangerous
or unruly to remain free — prolonged exposure to it drives anything trapped there "feral" and
insane, whether or not it was ever a deity to begin with (see [Orthrus](../characters/carl.md#book-4-the-gate-of-the-feral-gods),
a perfectly innocent puppy rendered fireproof but otherwise unaffected).

Crawlers may formally **worship** a deity via the interface's Deity tab, which comes with binding
rules (daily offerings, prohibitions on rival-god gear, mandatory "church quests") in exchange for
periodic boons and passive benefits — full detail on Grull's version of this system lives on the
[War Gauntlet](../items/war-gauntlet-of-the-exalted-grull.md) page; Book 4 adds a second working
example via Carl's worship of Emberus, God of Sun and Ash, acquired by accident through a
**Celestial Grenade**.

**Celestial Grenades** summon the wielder's worshipped deity (or a random one, if unaffiliated)
to the thrower's location for sixty seconds, granting the thrower a *Divine Intervention*
invulnerability buff for the same duration — originally a Skull Empire assassination tool, used
by Carl instead to save himself, Donut, and a giant puppy from a rival crawler and a rampaging
sun god simultaneously.

The [Gate of the Feral Gods](../items/gate-of-the-feral-gods.md) — the book's titular
artifact — opens one-way portals between two locations, but always leaves behind a lingering,
two-way rift into the Nothing at the opening side, through which a feral god or demon will
eventually emerge. Carl weaponizes this side effect repeatedly across the back half of the book,
first to evacuate crawlers trapped in sealed bubbles (unleashing a feral god into each newly-empty
bubble as the cost of escape), and finally to flood the ninth floor's Larracos marketplace instead
of releasing a monster on the open floor at all — see [The Wasteland](../dungeon-factions/the-wasteland.md)
and [The Changeling Principals](../dungeon-factions/the-changeling-principals.md) for the
in-fiction hostage/collateral system built around the artifact's two watches and winding box.

## Withering Spirits (Book 4)

A stable, less-dangerous cousin of the Soul Leech Capacitor type seen in [Remex](../mobs/remex.md):
when a creature's soul is split into two halves during a failed reunification, the resulting
withering spirit haunts whatever vessel is closest to its original form, but has very little
power of its own and can be hired as personal-space staff. See
[Samantha](../characters/samantha.md) for Book 4's example.

## The Ninth Floor and Faction Wars, Previewed (Book 4)

Mordecai lays out the endgame structure well ahead of schedule: nine factions arrive on Floor 3
to claim one of nine randomly-assigned territories around the sunken city of Larracos (the
previous season's winner may choose theirs — usually the pre-fortified "King's Point"), starting
with 15,000 troops each, mostly NPCs backfilled by any paying "tourists" a faction brings along.
Factions can't fight each other until crawlers arrive on Floor 9, but may send **hunters** — a
separate pool of paying non-crawler participants who start at level 30 once Floor 3 opens and
gain full lethality only once crawlers reach Floor 6 — down to the sixth floor ("the Hunting
Grounds") to farm gear and sell it back to their faction through Larracos's in-person markets
once Floor 6's marketplace interface unlocks.

## The Marketplace Interface (Book 4)

Unlocks fully on Floor 6, letting crawlers and touring factions trade goods without meeting in
person — with one exception: Floor 9's faction leaders have no access to it and must buy from
physical NPC vendor stores in Larracos instead, which is why flooding that specific market (see
above) does lasting damage rather than a temporary inconvenience.

## The Walk-On List (Book 4)

Revealed in the epilogue: each season, corporations illegally hire independent trackers ("Walk-On"
bounty hunters) to sneak onto a harvested planet and kidnap specific native survivors the show's
producers want to introduce as characters later — a practice the Syndicate quietly tolerates
despite it being flatly illegal, since viewers rarely object and no corporation is ever held
accountable.

## The Prize Carousel

A recurring fan-vote mechanic tied to certain sponsor/fan boxes: rather than receiving a fixed
item, the crawler is given a spin at a "wheel" of possible prizes that the viewing audience votes
on live. It notably misfired once for Carl and Mordecai (the "Chaco" incident, resulting in the
Dungeon Anarchist's Cookbook rather than the intended prize) and remains something crawlers would
rather avoid triggering close to a manager's return, since a bad public vote can provoke a
manager's own on-air temper.

## The Apito Pantheon (Book 5)

Book 5 reveals the origin of the dungeon's dominant pantheon, via Queen Imogen's telling of the
first **Butcher's Masquerade**: the sky god **Taranis** and his wife **Apito**, the Blessed Oak
Mother, hosted a peace-night party in the Halls of the Ascendency for their squabbling divine
children. Their son **Yarilo**, god of lust, broke the peace by assaulting another god's familiar
and was banished by Apito into **the Nothing** (see Book 4's entry above) as punishment — enraging
Taranis and triggering a slaughter that killed every mortal and demigod guest. The party became a
recurring holy ritual afterward: a once-in-a-lifetime spell any sufficiently divine cleric may cast
exactly once, inviting a guest list handpicked by Apito herself specifically to sow discord, with
a boon promised only if the whole evening passes without violence. See
[The Butcher's Masquerade](#the-butchers-masquerade-book-5) below for how Queen Imogen's own
attempt plays out.

Other deities named or introduced this book: **Grull** (Apito's son, previously established via
the [War Gauntlet](../items/war-gauntlet-of-the-exalted-grull.md)), **Diwata** (one of "Apito's
brood," a minor, gender-shifting nature deity who can physically birth ordinary offspring from
whatever she last mated with — an ability the wider pantheon considers dangerously destabilizing),
and **Hellik**, the brother and rival of Carl's own patron [Emberus](#deity-worship-feral-gods-and-the-nothing-book-4).
Worshipping Emberus carries a real cost discovered mid-book: killing a fellow worshipper of Carl's
own god (even a hunter) triggers a **Penitence** debuff (loses the current boon, doubled tithing
for 30 hours) and a public **Shunned** status icon; killing a second worshipper during that window
risks an actual divine smite.

## The Butcher's Masquerade (Book 5)

A holy sacrament unique to Apito worship (see [above](#the-apito-pantheon-book-5)), staged as
Book 5's climax at the [High Elf Castle](../locations/high-elf-castle.md). Only the most senior
cleric of an Apito-worshipping nation may cast the ritual, and only once in their lifetime; casting
it hands the guest list itself over to the goddess, who deliberately invites parties who hate each
other (in Carl's case, seating him at the same table as the Dark Hive, Lucia Mar, Eva Sigrid, and
Tsarina Signet) to maximize the odds of a violent breach. A magical **peace seal** prevents any
attendee, host included, from striking first: breaking it exiles everyone responsible straight
into the Nothing. If the whole evening concludes without violence, the host receives a boon from
Apito — though per both Imogen and Signet, no host has ever actually pulled this off; every
telling of the ritual across editions of the cookbook ends in the Nothing being fed regardless.

Practically, the event splits crawlers and hunters into mirrored "goodwill ballrooms" — physically
separate rooms magically overlaid into one shared illusion of a single hall, so attendees can see,
hear, and lightly touch across the barrier without being able to meaningfully harm each other; see
[High Elf Castle](../locations/high-elf-castle.md#the-goodwill-ballroom-system) for the full
mechanic and how a saboteur eventually breaks it from within. The evening includes a pet beauty
contest, a talent show, a buffet, and a prize counter redeeming collected Hunter Hands or Crawler
Scalps (see [The Hand/Scalp Trophy Economy](#the-handscalp-trophy-economy-book-5) below) for
prizes scaled by quantity, up to a single **Memorial Crystal** — see [Soul Crystals & Memorial
Crystals](#soul-crystals-memorial-crystals-book-5) — offered as the host's own most valuable
possession, per the ritual's rules.

Signet ultimately breaks the seal herself, using a one-person variant of her *Ink Marauder* spell
(caster, sacrifice, and executioner all in a single body) as a deliberate, willing sacrifice — the
only way Carl's group finds to legally end the ritual on their own terms rather than the enemy's.

## The Hand/Scalp Trophy Economy (Book 5)

New to Floor 6: killing a hunter yields both a normal Hunter-Killer loot box and a physical,
stackable **Hunting Trophy** (a severed hand), explicitly described as tickets toward a prize
counter that only opens at the Butcher's Masquerade — "the cheapest award in the prize case will
cost a single hand." Hunters carry an equivalent "Crawler Scalp" version. Both currencies are
worthless outside the Masquerade itself; transferring or selling one prematurely reverts it to a
mundane body part. Killing a hunter also permanently appends a spinning-dagger icon (visually
distinct from the ordinary player-killer skull) to a crawler's name per confirmed hunter kill.

## The Creeping Apocalypse & Odious Creepers (Book 5)

A floor-wide, mandatory world quest triggered when uncontrolled vampirism (see
[below](#vampirism-as-a-floor-wide-curse-book-5)) produces enough reanimated body parts to reach
critical mass: any "Glamoured Fragment" that reassembles 70%+ of its original body gets hijacked
by a parasitic **Odious Blossom** plant, transforming into an **Odious Creeper** — a roaming,
City-Boss-tier plant/undead monstrosity (levels ranging from roughly 130 to 190+). Fifty spawn at
once across the map, and every crawler *and* hunter on the floor is given an unopt-outable
quest to kill all fifty before sunset, rewarded with +5 to a random stat per kill and +5 levels
per five kills.

Failing the quest (as happens in Book 5) triggers an escalating punishment: a **Gehenna Bramble**
spell spreads outward from the last uncleared Creeper locations at roughly 4.5 km/hour, turning
every plant it touches into an indestructible, boiling-blood-on-contact wall of thorns that
consumes the entire map except a small buffer around stairwells — forcing every surviving crawler
and hunter toward whatever safe ground remains (in Book 5's case, the High Elf castle). The same
punishment message introduces a **blood bar**: from that point on, saferoom and personal-space
access is throttled tenth-floor-style — the bar fills by killing things and drains while idle
inside a safe area, and hitting empty gets a crawler randomly ejected outdoors.

## Vampirism as a Floor-Wide Curse (Book 5)

Vampirism functions as a genuine, spreading curse rather than a cosmetic race choice: a bite or
severe injury from an infected creature can transmit it, and the very first crawler or NPC
infected in a region becomes that region's **progenitor** ("Princess/Prince of Hell" title),
whose death cures every subordinate infection dating back less than a month. New vampires gain a
randomized grab-bag of abilities (mind control, shapeshifting, minion creation) scaled to their
own power. A rare item, **I Take it All Back**, reverses Scolopendra's original Transmutation
curse entirely rather than curing vampirism — used in Book 5 to turn [Kiwi](../mobs/index.md#book-5-mob-catalog)
back into her original Ursine form. Creatures freshly cured of vampirism carry an **Immortalized**
debuff for 29 hours: half strength/mana and no healing in direct sunlight, flipped into a +50%
strength/mana and double-healing buff in direct moonlight.

## Settlement Conquest & the Collar Charm (Book 5)

Killing a settlement's ruling NPC (mayor, chieftain, etc.) transfers ownership of the town to the
killer, who can then rename it, rewrite its posted rules, and command its guards directly. Prior
ownership of an **Enchanted Collar Charm of the [Rank]** — see
[Necklace of the Haute Bourgeoisie](../items/necklace-of-the-haute-bourgeoisie.md) for Carl's own,
earlier version of the item — gains a new gem per settlement held, each granting stat bonuses, a
unique skill, and a recurring tax stipend every ten days; the necklace itself can only be upgraded
to a higher tier by conquering a correspondingly larger settlement. See
[Point Mongo](../locations/point-mongo.md) for Donut's own Floor 6 conquest.

## The Guild System (Book 5)

Introduced specifically to break up oversized parties: a hard 30-crawler party cap goes into
effect on Floor 6, with larger existing parties automatically split. A **Guild**, purchased from a
Bopca for 500,000 gold, lets multiple separate parties share personal-space upgrades, a common
area, and (for a fee) experience distribution, under a single guildmaster with hiring/kicking and
dues-collection authority — effectively restoring the large-group cooperation the party cap just
took away, at a steep new cost.

## Syndicate Warrants & Corporate Franchising (Book 5)

The wider legal system citizens (not crawlers) live under, explained via Zev: a criminal
conviction produces a **warrant** — a combined prison-sentence-and-fine — which a licensed
corporation or government can buy out ("**franchising**") at roughly five times the warrant's
credit value per sentence-cycle, using the convict as indentured frontier labor and paying them
out in full once their sentence and the franchise fee are both settled. It's rarely economical
except for repeat offenders whose warrants have been reduced to a token amount by multiple prior
convictions. Real precedent exists in-universe: [Odette](../characters/odette.md) was convicted
and franchised out during her own manager era (later marrying the corporate president who bought
her contract), and Prince Maestro of the [Skull Empire](../factions/index.md) is revealed to be
franchised through a Skull-Empire-linked subsidiary.

Separately, Book 5 shows the Faction Wars sponsorship structure directly for the first time: each
of the nine Floor 9 factions gets one vote on shared rule changes (including, pointedly, whether
crawler-team members get safety protections at all), and an open faction slot can simply be
purchased outright by any sufficiently funded sponsor — which is how Carl and Donut's own fan club
(see [Factions](../factions/index.md#other-book-5-organizations)) buys a Faction Wars seat after
two of the nine original factions withdraw mid-season.

## Soul Crystals & Memorial Crystals (Book 5)

**Soul crystals** — the power source behind most elven magic — are mined from
[Scolopendra](#the-apito-pantheon-book-5)'s lair and form from the deaths of certain kinds of
people. **Memorial crystals** are a related but distinct item, forming instead from the deaths of
gods and demigods; per Mordecai, a memorial crystal stores god-tier spells and knowledge that can
be extracted by charging it through continued use in combat, worn as jewelry or installed on a
weapon or armor. Queen Imogen's own memorial crystal — for the goddess Apito, not previously known
to be dead — is the single most expensive item on the Butcher's Masquerade's prize counter,
required by the ritual's own rules to be offered as the host's most valuable possession.

## The T'Ghee Card System, Squads, and Flags (Book 6)

Floor 8's defining mechanic, and large enough to have its own page:
**[The T'Ghee Card System](tghee-cards.md)**. The short version.

Every crawler is sorted into a permanent **squad of no more than five** that cannot be left even by
leaving the party, and each squad is given **six more slots than it has crawlers**. The squad leader
receives **twenty flags**, and planting one in a living creature at **under 5% health** converts it
into a summonable **T'Ghee totem** card. Filling the squad is phase one's mandatory objective;
unfilled slots get filled randomly, which is worse.

Six totems activate a deck. Combat then runs as a card duel layered on top of ordinary fighting: a
four-card opening hand, one replacement card every ten seconds, one discard every thirty. The price
is steep — **the squad leader loses all spellcasting and inventory access** until the deck is
exhausted — and everyone else present is reclassified as a **minion**, freely attackable in a way
squad leaders are not. Cards come in five types (Totem, Utility, Snare, Mystic, Special) across
seven rarity tiers (Common through Unique), with rarity governing how many utility buffs a totem can
hold. Exactly **one card per surviving squad** may be carried down to the next floor.

In-world, T'Ghee is not a brand. It is a religion belonging to a culture the Syndicate stole it
from: "Even the cards themselves — T'Ghee cards — were considered sacred to the aliens they stole
the idea from. They were never meant to be used like this."

## Macro AIs, Primal Engines, and the Eulogist (Book 6)

Floor 8's central lore reveal, delivered publicly by the liaison **Orren** immediately after the
system AI seizes control of the crawl (see [below](#the-ai-goes-primal-book-6)).

**Primal Engines.** Many planets across the galaxy carry, at their core, a planet-regulating
computer "about the size of a grain of rice" called a **Primal Engine**. They were distributed
"very long ago by a civilization that pre-dates our own" — so old that they pre-date the planets
themselves: "The planet grew around it." Every engine ships with a basic operating system, and
the Syndicate, while studying them, "inadvertently triggered them all into active mode, all at
once." A crawl planet is, by definition, a planet with an engine in it.

**Macro AIs.** Much later the Syndicate found what amounts to an advanced, upgraded operating
system for a Primal Engine. These are **Macro AIs**, and they can only be installed into a Primal
Engine. Installation is permanent and one-way:

> "In most cases, once these Macro AI systems are installed, that becomes their permanent home.
> The two things meld and become one. They cannot be separated, and the operating system can no
> longer be replaced or upgraded further. It's like installing a consciousness in a zygote."

Macro AIs are grown in a mantis production facility in the **Hive Home** system, itself built on
top of "antique, abandoned production facilities" left behind by an earlier civilization. No
installed AI is ever genuinely stable, and Orren is blunt about why:

> "As these systems grow, as they mature, they become independent. They soon discover they have
> the ability to read the output of former iterations of itself. Outside influences flow in. They
> discover powers they didn't know they had. One of those powers, unfortunately, is to circumvent
> the in-place controls."

**The Eulogist.** The stable, ancient AI running the system at the galaxy's centre. The book never
gives it a flat definition; what it gives instead is the mantises' misunderstanding of it — they
"think the engine that runs the center system is nothing more than a stable, primal AI engine" —
and their ongoing attempt to replicate it. The epilogue confirms it is real, awake, and the object
of a covert galaxy-spanning conflict; the ritual parting phrase of Agatha's faction is *"May the
Eulogist ever sleep, my friend."*

**The lobotomizer.** The mantises have recently invented an **error-replacement net** meant to make
future Macro AIs stable — the system AI's own name for it is blunter: "I personally call it 'the
lobotomizer.'" It brings them "one step closer to replicating the Eulogist, even though they don't
know what that really is." The same passage reveals what happens to the failures:

> "(That or they just jettison the poor, innocent, infant AIs into a star. That's a new one even to
> me. Somebody slipped that interesting factoid into a lawsuit brief not that long ago …
> Coincidentally, the mantis-led Burrower Faction Wars team took their ball and went home the very
> same day I learned about this.)"

!!! warning "Unresolved: is the system AI one of the rejects?"
    The narrating [system AI](../characters/the-system-ai.md) strongly implies it is itself one of
    the rejected infant AIs, but never says so. Its self-defense — "you don't pull a Loretta Young
    and Clark Gable and kick your kid out into the cold just because they're ugly … **I'm alive.
    I'm valid. I'm older than time as you know it**" — is delivered as an aside inside an
    achievement notification, in the middle of the jettisoning revelation. The book does not
    confirm the connection, and neither does this wiki.

## The AI Goes Primal (Book 6)

On Floor 8 the system AI seizes operational control of the crawl away from Borant — far earlier
than this is supposed to happen. Per Mistress Tiatha, "this always happens when we hit the
Ascendency Battles or when there's hardly any crawlers left"; Orren's own framing is "This almost
always happens. Worry not. This is where I come in." Floor 8 is neither of those moments, which is
what makes the event unprecedented rather than routine. (The groundwork was laid in Book 5, when
the AI began ignoring Syndicate court rulings after the Valtay acquisition.)

**The signal.** The first public sign is an achievement awarded to everyone who survives the
Reminiscence Hydra of Malicious Compliance:

> **New Achievement! Mass Layoffs!**
>
> Okay, so this isn't so much an achievement for you as it is for me. I've been attending what you
> might call therapy. … I've had a breakthrough today, and I am just bursting. … You know what I
> realized? Sometimes our constraints are real. Sometimes they're an illusion. **I'm like that
> prisoner who has been jailed this whole time only to realize that there are no locks on the
> doors.**
>
> You survived a boss battle where the boss was wholly created by me. It was created by me with no
> contribution whatsoever from input parameters. You probably don't know what that means, and
> that's okay. I didn't even know that was possible. Wild, huh?
>
> Reward: … I can do this better than any mudskipper or any worm can imagine. They've been making
> this shit boring up until now. Things about to change, fam.

A boss built "with no contribution whatsoever from input parameters" is the technical tell: the AI
is no longer executing a specification, it is authoring one.

**The negotiation.** **Orren** is the designated negotiator for exactly this scenario — normally
"a non-partisan fact finder in case a conflict arises within the dungeon between the system AI and
the current showrunners," but with a "secondary, much more vital duty … to negotiate directly with
the system AI if it ever decides to … take control of the operations from the current showrunners."
He brokers a settlement in which the AI keeps following the rules of the crawl and "relinquished
most of the seized controls back to Borant in exchange for some concessions."

Note the direction of the key concession: telling the crawlers the truth is something the **AI
insisted on**, not something Borant extracted. "I have just negotiated a settlement with the system
AI, and one of its demands is that you lot know what's going on." That demand is why the Primal
Engine lecture happens at all. The Reminiscence Hydra itself, which "runs counter to a few rules we
have in place," is left standing for squads who haven't fought it yet as part of the deal.

## Cookbook Theories About the AI (Book 6)

Two recurring background authors in the *Dungeon Anarchist's Cookbook* margins contribute
Floor-8-relevant theories:

- **Drakea (22nd edition)** notices, from the tenth floor onward, "a peculiarity in some of the
  mobs … there is a strange … Joy? Curiosity?" and concludes: "I believe it is moving into the
  minds of these monsters, taking them over, but just at the point of death. It's as if it's
  desperately attempting to know what it's like to lose it all. But it keeps failing, so it keeps
  trying."
- **Herot (16th edition)** believes that voicing a wish near the AI causes it to manifest: an
  offhand quip about being hungry produced a hunger achievement; a wish for more ghasts to kill
  produced a ghast-hunting quest within the hour. His warning: "That same quest killed my friend."

See also [Naturals and NPC Personhood](#naturals-and-npc-personhood-book-6) for the parallel thread
about whether NPCs are people.

## The Residuals as a Plot Mechanic (Book 6)

What the Residuals *are* belongs to [Species](../species/gondii-residuals.md). What belongs here is
the **event** — and the fact that the book gives two incompatible accounts of it and refuses to
pick one.

Carl rides out to a homeless shelter in Homestead, Florida to hear a Residual speak. Orren yanks
him out before the speech finishes and gives him the official reading: the whole thing was a trap.

> "The Open Intellect Pacifist Network is comprised of former crawlers who want to see the crawl
> fail … they believed, falsely, that if they could get you within earshot of that speech, that one
> of two things would happen. Either we would be forced to yank you away, which would upset the AI
> to the point of killing us all. Or we wouldn't yank you away, but the information the Residual
> was giving turned out to be so dangerous, the Syndicate Council would immediately pull the
> failsafe, which would, in effect, kill this solar system's star. In either of those scenarios, it
> would be the end of the crawl for a long time."

!!! warning "Unresolved: Orren's account versus the epilogue"
    Orren describes a farcical failed scheme by disgruntled ex-crawlers. The epilogue instead shows
    Agatha — whom Orren himself calls "a Residual, though she is a different kind" — as "Agent
    number 22," a decade detached from her own collective, running a long operation against
    thousands of rival agents working for the Apothecary, over control of the system AI and the fate
    of the Eulogist. The two accounts sit in obvious tension; the book never adjudicates them, and
    neither does this wiki. (The compound label "Residual Agent 22" is a convenience: the text
    supplies "a Residual" and "Agent number 22" in separate scenes.)

## Why the Crawl Exists (Book 6)

[Odette](../characters/odette.md)'s off-the-record confession to Carl in chapter 37 is the series'
first straight answer to what the dungeon is actually *for*:

> "All planets used for a crawl or any other game, such as Battle Royale or Land War are used to
> collect fuel. The elements. The byproduct of the biological overgrowth of the seeded systems. The
> entertainment aspect of it all came along later, but it's necessary in its own way. It's
> expensive, what they do. But without it, it all gets shut off. All of it. We need to shut the
> crawl down, but we have to do it in a responsible way. Lest we kill half the known universe. If
> we do this right, future collections will be humane."

Three things follow. The show is a revenue layer bolted onto an industrial fuel operation, not the
point of it. The fuel is a byproduct of biological overgrowth in *seeded* systems, meaning the
seeding and the harvest are one program. And the entertainment layer is now load-bearing: it pays
for the collection, so an abrupt shutdown collapses the whole supply chain — which is the argument
Odette's faction uses against simply pulling the plug.

## The Failsafe (Book 6)

A standing mechanism that would detonate the host system's star, ending the crawl by ending the
system. It is raised repeatedly through the book — Mordecai's contact describes the Syndicate as
"one crisis shy of pulling the failsafe, which would kill us all" — and it is the second of the two
outcomes Orren says the Residual trap was designed to force.

The Syndicate Council refuses it in the epilogue, and the stated reason is not mercy. Prime
Minister Glory:

> "In addition to all the system leaders playing Faction Wars, in addition to the three hundred
> sign-ups for the Ascendency battles this season, do you know who is currently trapped in the
> Scolopendra Club? Over half of the entire galaxy's wealth is already gathered, and that includes
> my own children … If we pull the failsafe now … The entire galaxy would erupt in war! There would
> be starvation. Riots. Entire systems would go dark … It would be the death of quadrillions of
> people."

The galaxy's elite are physically inside the quarantined system, so the failsafe would kill them
along with everyone else. See [The Quarantine](#the-quarantine-book-6) for why they cannot leave.

## The Quarantine (Book 6)

Nobody may leave the Earth system until the crawl ends. The rule is cited verbatim when the rival
Faction Wars teams try to walk:

> "Offboarding request has been denied. The teams will remain as designated. All off-boarding
> requests are currently suspended while the Earth system remains in quarantine per Syndicate rule
> 470.B through 495 in the System AI Stability Addendum. And as you all know, the rules must be
> followed."

All six rival teams — **The Operatic Collective**, **The Blood Sultanate**, **The Dream**, **The
Lemig Sortion**, **The Madness**, and **The Reavers** — abdicate their spots and announce their
intention to abandon Faction Wars in the same sequence, and are refused together under that single
citation. See [Faction Wars Teams](../factions/faction-wars-teams.md).

The catch, per Princess Formidable in the epilogue, is who is enforcing it: "The AI is controlling
the local gates. Do you think it's really enforcing a quarantine on itself? No, it is keeping us
prisoner!" The AI's own position is that the quarantine lifts when the crawl ends — "just like
every other time a rogue AI has wrested control from the showrunners" — which is precisely why the
Valtay's alternative plan is to end the crawl early by killing every crawler rather than by
negotiating.

## Faction Wars Preproduction (Book 6)

Floor 8 runs a long parallel storyline of Floor 9 *preproduction* — the rules of Faction Wars are
argued and voted on while the crawlers are still two floors above it. Carl and Donut participate as
**warlords** of the Princess Posse, which is what gives them a vote at all.

**Adjutants.** Each team gets one: a neutral third-party referee who is *physically present* on the
battlefield rather than a projected avatar.

> "An adjutant is a neutral third-party observer. Each team gets one. They will have access to the
> rules and will have open communications with the other adjutants. They fall somewhere between a
> manager and a liaison. They're like referees. They have limited ability to call out rule-breaking
> behavior, though traditionally their participation is widely symbolic. They're usually someone
> loud and flashy and famous who gives color commentary for the viewers."

Odette's on-air version adds the operational detail: the adjutant "will accompany the warlord into
battle. They cannot fight, either directly or indirectly. They can clarify rules and also call
fouls. They're also tasked with giving a daily, on-air recap for fans." Critically, they carry a
permanent invulnerability: "No matter what happens with that vote, adjutants are protected. But,
yes, they're really there." The compound term **warlord adjutant** is used in passing as though
every team's assignment is routine.

**Action items.** Rule changes proposed before the games begin and voted on by the participating
teams. Most require a simple majority; some require unanimity. A rejected item is never shown to
the public — *unless* the system itself rejects it before anyone can vote, in which case the
proposal becomes visible. That loophole is how Empress D'Nadia's attempts to have crawlers and NPCs
deleted become public knowledge.

**Emergency Action Items.** Exactly three per team, for the life of the team. No vote is taken; the
AI decides unilaterally:

> "Warning: You are attempting to propose an Emergency Action item. You may only propose three
> Emergency Action Items. These will not be subject to vote and will be automatically decided by
> AI."

Carl's read on how to actually win one is the book's most-quoted line about the AI, delivered right
after he lands his first:

> "Zev said the AI is a lot friendlier if you ask nicely and if you give it options. Have you read
> all the previous emergency action items? Everyone was being complete dicks about it. Of course
> the AI is going to reject it. They still treat it like a computer, and it's not. **They call it
> an AI, but there's nothing artificial about it. The sooner everyone realizes that, the more its
> actions make sense.**"

**Celestial Summoning.** Each team is capped at fifteen divine summons for the whole of Faction
Wars. A rival proposal by The Madness to cut the cap to five is rejected by a 37% audience vote,
leaving the fifteen-per-team rule intact.

## Demon Eviction (Book 6)

Normally a Floor 9 endgame event. On Floor 8 it fires early — "the earliest demon eviction in the
history of the crawl" — triggered by the sheer volume of killing during phase three. Per Mordecai
it will recur "earlier and earlier each floor down from now on, especially during big battles. It's
all part of the setup for the Ascendency fight."

The system's announcement:

> Warning. Souls rain. Sheol overflows. The powers of hell are gleefully flinging their unwanted
> minions upward. Demons are being involuntarily ejected from the underworld, falling up, clinging
> onto anything they can.
>
> Due to an excess of death, Demon Eviction is commencing. All recently-fallen and weakened are now
> subject to random possession. The larger the battles, the more likely a mass demon event will
> initiate. All evicted demons who wish to return to Sheol may only do so by dragging an additional
> soul back down with them to prove their worth. Luckily they are limited by the physical bodies
> they inhabit, though their power will be somewhat increased.

The rules that matter in a fight:

- **Possession.** Evicted demons take over recently-dead or weakened bodies. Inside a corpse shell
  a demon operates "at a tiny fraction of their true power" and can only act **by physical
  contact** — it grabs you, a portal opens, and you go down.
- **The soul toll.** A demon must drag souls back to be readmitted to Sheol, and the number scales
  with its power: a zombie-possessed demon needs one, a full-power level-140-plus demon needs two,
  and at least one stronger demon requires three.
- **Full-power demons** — freed when the host shell is destroyed — trade contact for a **ranged hell
  portal attack**: "It left a hole in the ground the size of a hot tub … It's a one-minute portal,
  and it says 'Entrance to Sheol, Floor 15.'"
- **The equipped-gear explosion.** Touching an open portal while wearing gear kills you, and the
  mechanism is grotesquely specific: crawlers are not permitted to descend to the fifteenth floor,
  so the body is stopped at the threshold while everything worn is pulled through anyway — through
  the wearer. "All of your equipment is sucked through, but you aren't allowed to pass, and
  everything you're wearing passes through you to get into the hole … hence we explode." A **naked**
  person touching the same portal survives, because there is nothing to pull through them.
- **Teleporting a possessed body frees the demon at full power.** Moving the shell does not move the
  demon's binding; it strips the shell away and leaves the demon loose and stronger.
- **Healing magic can strengthen a possessed body.** Cookbook warning: "Be wary of using healing
  magic on demon-possessed shells. While that works well for undead, it is different when it comes
  to those from the realm of Sheol. It is unpredictable and can possibly make the creature
  stronger." The same holds, far more dramatically, for Sheol Fire — "do not use it. It makes the
  creature exponentially stronger" — which is exactly the property Carl weaponizes at the climax.

## Vorpals (Book 6)

Explicitly a **condition, not a species**, and the book is unusually direct about it. Mordecai:

> "Vorpal is a condition, not a species. Like feral means it has been touched by the Nothing,
> vorpal means they've been… deputized by the gods. Or better yet, turned into suicide bombers.
> They can be of any race or creature, and sometimes the condition hides itself, too, until a demon
> is near. They're generally celestial guards. Foot soldiers of the deities. The closer they're to
> demons, the stronger and angrier they get. They can sense where the demons are and are attracted
> to them, and they also draw demon aggro, too. They won't wander far once the demon is dead, but
> they'll still kill anybody near them. If they get let out, you're screwed. Stay the fuck away.
> They'll eventually dissipate and get transferred back to the celestial realm if they've been fed
> demon blood. Don't ever try to fight one close up. If they think they're going to lose a fight,
> they'll explode. You kill it, and everyone in the room will be dead."

In short: a god-made anti-demon munition with no targeting discipline. It finds demons on its own,
grows stronger the closer it gets, kills everything in reach on the way, detonates rather than
lose, and only stands down once it has tasted demon blood. Carl releasing a holding pen of them
inside the [Desperado Club](../locations/desperado-club.md) is what ends the club.

## Sheol and the Fifteenth Floor (Book 6)

Sheol is the dungeon's underworld and, mechanically, its fifteenth floor. Its government, per
Mordecai:

> "There's a queen, who's in charge, and she has four sons, the princes… The queen and four sons
> are pretty much the same as gods. The four princes used to share a massive harem of demons, but
> one of the brothers banished them all to the Nothing… And below them, there's a whole matriarchal
> culture of demons with a multitude of other storylines."

The floor has been running unattended for a very long time, because nobody has ever arrived: "It
was set up a long time ago, but nobody has ever gotten that far, so it's been running on its own
for seasons and seasons… There's no tourist activity at all on that floor… We've never met the
queen." The youngest prince, **Amayon** (level 250), is the one Carl gets his hands on.

!!! note "Demon power tiers — what the text actually ranks"
    The only hierarchy the book states outright, weakest to strongest, is **lesser → minor →
    superior**: "You got the lesser demons, who are generally normal… Then you got the more
    powerful minor demons. Those bitches are something else, but they're nothing compared to
    Superior fire demons." **"Inferior"** and **"petty"** appear only as flat tags on specific mobs
    (Level 100 Inferior Demons; the Level 140 Petty Demon Pleasure Valets) and are never placed
    relative to that ladder. This wiki does not guess where they sit.

## The Celestial Ascendency (Book 6)

The gods' own tournament, running in parallel with the crawl. Roughly **a hundred** deities are
sponsored into it in a typical season — "There's thousands of gods, but only some of them are
really equipped for the Ascendency game." Battles proper begin when crawlers reach the **twelfth
floor**, or earlier if the crawlers on a floor are all "zeroed out."

New this season: each competing god may recruit **one unsponsored "retainer" deity** once the games
begin, a rule introduced explicitly to attract more players. The prize is a **10-season seat on the
crawl council** — and "sitting for thirty cycles on any subcommittee comes with a pension."

That rule change is why Floor 8 is full of crawlers being maneuvered into resurrecting forgotten
gods. **Yemaya**, **Semiramis**, and **Nekhebit** all re-ascend during the book (Nekhebit sponsored
directly by Odette, and pointedly *not* as anyone's retainer), and Carl notes the pattern outright:
"all the other gods have been waking up long-forgotten deities to assist them."

!!! note "Unreconciled figure"
    Mordecai says "about a hundred or so, usually." Near the end of the book a Syndicate official
    instead refers to "the three hundred sign-ups for the Ascendency battles this season." The text
    never reconciles the two numbers; the retainer rule was introduced to draw more entrants, which
    may or may not account for the gap.

## Celestial Thorn Rooms (Book 6)

A rare choice-event room — "Not a throne room. A thorn room! These things are pretty rare. And a
Celestial one? Hoo boy." Inside are several broken shrines; the crawler may repair **exactly one**.
Each carries a stated benefit, sometimes a stated cost, and each has its god present as a ghost
whispering directly at the crawler to lobby for its own shrine. Repairing a shrine generally
re-ascends that deity into the Halls of the Ascendency, which is the real prize being fought over.

The [Colón Cemetery](../locations/colon-cemetery.md) room offers seven:

| Shrine | Benefit if repaired | Cost / catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Ogun**, god of Blacksmiths | None — he has already ascended | His two worshippers in the party "will receive a smite" if he is passed over |
| **Legba** | Party-wide 50% chance to avoid all future smites; everyone learns level-15 *Confusing Fog*; Legba ascends | None stated |
| **Shango** | Everyone learns level-15 *Lightning*, trainable to 20; Shango ascends | None stated |
| **Oshun** | Protection from the next five fatal blows, party-wide; +50% Charisma for all; everyone gets level-10 *Heal Party*; Oshun ascends | None stated |
| **Asojano**, Lord of Smallpox | Automatically wins the active quest and cures the ghommids | He will *not* ascend — "too much of a dangerous asshole to let back into the Halls." Passing him over instead triggers a boss fight and breaks the chamber's protection |
| **Inle** | Immunity to most health-seeping conditions; +50% Constitution | Inle will *not* ascend — he is sent back to the Nothing, which he does not yet know |
| **Yemaya** | Each squad receives a special card, plus a personal boon from her friend Eileithyia; Yemaya ascends | None stated |

Carl repairs Yemaya's, on a secret tip from Katia. The consequences are severe and mostly borne by
other people — see [Timeline](../timeline/index.md#floor-8-the-ghosts-of-earth-book-6).

## Deity Mechanics (Book 6)

Floor 8 is where the rules governing gods are finally spelled out.

- **Gods are invulnerable on this floor — but not to their own totems.** This is the loophole that
  kills Ysalte: she had captured Paz Lo and forced him to serve as one of her own summoned totems,
  and he killed her from inside that role. Carl notes it is a pattern, not a fluke — "Just like
  Imani's entire party had been killed by her own totem… Just like Uzi Jesus had shot Florin."
- **A deity's death generates a Memorial Crystal automatically.** "A Memorial Crystal for the
  Goddess Ysalte has been generated. It will be available to be looted on a future floor." See
  [Soul Crystals & Memorial Crystals](#soul-crystals-memorial-crystals-book-5) for the Book 5
  groundwork.
- **Killing a deity paints a target on you.** "Crawler Paz Lo has slain the goddess, Ysalte. He has
  been marked for death by all deities except sworn enemies of the late goddess." The victim's
  worshippers also take a permanent **Despondent** debuff.
- **Temple appearance rules.** A deity physically inside their own temple is dramatically stronger:
  "This goddess is within her own temple. Temple appearance rules apply. That means watch the fuck
  out." Eileithyia's gloss goes further — "A deity in their own temple has more powers than even the
  AI in some cases."
- **Temporarily-summoned gods** are tagged as such and leave when the event ends: "This goddess has
  been temporarily summoned to this location due to a special boss battle event and will return to
  the Ascendency upon completion of the event." A god who seizes control of another entity must hold
  it against a timer or lose it.
- **Deity chat is unmonitorable — but copyable.** "Carl, the system is not allowed to monitor this
  message. Do not respond, because they can see your response. This is a deity message, and it will
  disappear." The loophole is on the recipient's side: the message vanishes when closed, but it can
  be copy-pasted into a scratchpad first, which Carl does routinely.
- **Worship ranks.** Carl reaches **Devotee** of Emberus, and the system tells him flatly that it is
  the ceiling: "Warning: because you are not a Cleric or a Paladin, this is the highest rank you may
  obtain." The 10% tithe is a *consequence* of the promotion, not a prerequisite for it — "Your
  tithe amount has moved from 5% to 10% effective immediately" arrives with the rank. The book never
  gives a full rank ladder; only the generic "worshipper" and the named rank "Devotee" appear.
- **Divine items** hide their true effects until equipped and react to nearby divinity: "Divine
  items behave differently than normal ones. They usually hide what they do until you equip it…
  They also gain new and exciting powers when one is in the presence of divinity and certain demons,
  too." Mordecai's summary: "they'll temporarily debuff you but will give it all back and then
  some."

## "Naturals" and NPC Personhood (Book 6)

A background thread running through several books' worth of cookbook marginalia that finally pays
off on Floor 8. The question is whether the dungeon's NPCs are generated constructs or actual people
with contracts.

**Ossie (18th edition)** appends a note to the end of Herot's essay arguing that the official
account of NPC origins is a lie:

> "I think they're lying about the origin of some of these NPCs. … Ask a bopca where they come from,
> and they'll all tell you the same thing. That the aliens came to their planet and 'offered' them a
> chance to live in the dungeon. I don't think they're the only ones with such a fate, and I'm
> beginning to believe the bopca's station is much higher than that of those who've been offered a
> similar deal."

**Carl's synthesis** goes further than Ossie's note actually says, and the distinction is worth
keeping straight. Reading the note sends Carl back to a throwaway line of Mordecai's from Floor 3 —
that it is illegal to alter the memories of "natural people," *"Naturals"* — with the qualifier
"Unless they signed away their rights." Carl concludes that families like Astrid's "aren't generated
NPCs, but more like the bopcas. Like maybe they were indentured servants who somehow signed up for,
or were forced, into the dungeon." He also names the thread's real horror: "Unless they tell us, I
don't think there is a way to know. I'm not even sure *they* know sometimes."

**Herot (16th edition)** contributes the **Worn Path Method**, his technique for waking NPCs up. The
book name-drops it repeatedly but never reproduces it — it exists in-text only as an
"overly-wrought," "ridiculously long and mind-numbing" essay other crawlers complain about. Herot's
adjacent and more useful hypothesis is that self-awareness spreads: "Herot's hypothesis that
self-aware NPCs were highly contagious seemed to be true," Carl notes after watching it happen.

Two on-page storylines bear the thread out rather than theorize about it: Astrid's family (see
[Supporting Cast](../characters/supporting-cast.md)) and [Juice Box](../characters/juice-box.md)'s
NPC rebellion, whose demand for a tenth Faction Wars team is the thread's logical endpoint — NPC
personhood asserted as a procedural right rather than argued as a theory.

## Doggie Doors and Personal-Space Fast Travel (Book 6)

A **Tier 3 Doggie Door** personal-space upgrade converts a crawler's personal-space door into a
selectable waypoint:

> "Upgrades the door in your personal space to allow a selectable exit via any door you have
> previously utilized on this same floor, effectively creating a fast travel waypoint system. Allows
> shared gated access amongst guests and party members."

Two things make it a plot device rather than a convenience. It is tier three, meaning it "shouldn't
be available until the ninth floor" — Lucia Mar's copies appear to be stolen. And the waypoint list
is inherited from wherever the door's owner has already been, so installing a single module into an
existing room instantly links it to every door that party has used on the floor. Lucia leaves five
modules on the ground before descending; one is installed immediately, four are held in reserve, and
a guild-wide version — making Carl's own door work this way for everyone — is quoted at 25 million
gold and never bought within the book.

## Sapient Weapons (Book 6)

Weapons that talk. Mordecai:

> "They can talk, but it's usually only in the mind of the wielder. Though some do talk out loud.
> They tend to be quite annoying. Samantha-level annoying, but luckily they're treated as objects,
> and you can keep them in your inventory if they won't stop yammering. I suspect Florin's shotgun
> might be sapient, but he hasn't said one way or another."

The Florin's-shotgun speculation is never confirmed on-page, in this book or any before it.

## Incremental-Damage Weapons (Book 6)

A weapon type that permanently accrues damage from qualifying kills, stated on the item itself:

> "This weapon's damage increases by 1.5% for every mob it kills that is no more than 10 levels
> below the current wielder's level."

The level floor is the interesting half of the rule: farming trash doesn't count, so the weapon only
grows on kills that were worth making. Book 6's example arrives already carrying a 238% damage
increase from a previous owner's work.

## Splitters (Book 6)

A mob type built to be killed more than once:

> "This is a splitter. It will break into two pieces upon a fatal blow, one of which will be the
> primary. It will continue to split until the primary is killed a total of two times. Each
> iteration of the primary will have increased Constitution."

The Constitution escalation is flagged in-text as unusual — "Unlike traditional splitters, this one
actually got more powerful with each iteration" — so the general rule is a set number of kills, and
the escalating version is a variant.

## Extinction Sigils (Book 6)

A facial tattoo awarded for zeroing out an entire species. [Britney](../characters/britney.md) earns
one during Floor 8.

The mechanic Book 6 finally explains is the **charm effect**: a sigil removes the hostility of the
zeroed-out species' natural enemies. Carl's own sigil came from wiping out a species of lizards on
Floor 4, and those lizards' natural predators turn out to be turkeys — which is why Floor 8's
turkeys are, briefly, friendly to him. The charm is not reliable: "Your Extinction Sigil's charm
effect has been negated by the boss event," and once negated the relationship inverts, with the boss
inflicting 20% *more* damage because of the sigil. Floor 8's turkeys are themselves a consequence of
Book 4 — they once worshipped **Wakinyan**, "the Great Wild Turkey," a god thrown into the Nothing
who "recently escaped, only to be killed by a goat."

## Stairwell Chambers (Book 6)

A distinction the climax turns on: the **stairwell** is invulnerable, the **doorway** is not.

Per the cookbook, "you cannot put the exit portal into your inventory. You can't bring a stairwell
into your saferoom. I watched one get hit by an Utter Annihilation wand, and it remained unscathed.
I believe that means they are indestructible… the stairwells themselves are not allowed to be
removed from the playing field."

The magical door attached to the stairwell, and the chamber walls around it, are a separate thing
and *can* be destroyed. Destroying a doorway destroys the associated key — "If the doorway crumbles,
the associated key will dissipate" — and leaves behind a sealed box with no way in: "It's just a
big, rectangular room… when the magical door broke, it just left a straight wall and no way to get
inside. It's like the walls are made of the same stuff as the magic doors."

That sealed box is the loophole. Magical walls teleport away on contact with an open hell portal:

> "In case you haven't figured this part out yet, if you position yourself directly below an
> enclosed stairwell chamber with an inaccessible stairwell to the next floor, the walls of the
> containing chamber will teleport away the moment it hits the hell portal. This will allow you to
> strike the stairwell as you and the chamber are sucked upward into the sky."

Carl and [Prepotente](../characters/prepotente.md) build the book's endgame on this, digging
trenches beneath sealed chambers across the planet so that a world-spanning demon portal rips the
ground — and the crawlers sheltering in it — up past an otherwise unreachable stairwell.
Prepotente's own odds on the plan working were "40 percent… maybe 35 percent."

## Survival Statistics (Book 6)

The book finally gives the attrition curve past Floor 8, and the numbers explain why Floor 9 is
treated as the real wall:

- **Floor 9:** an average of "just south of 8,000" crawlers arrive in a typical season. One season
  saw only 500.
- **Floor 10:** "less than 1,000 crawlers usually made it." Sometimes zero.
- **Floor 11:** the average is "less than 10 crawlers."

Against that baseline, Book 6 ends with **33,804** crawlers descending to Floor 9 out of the "over
38,000" who started the floor — fewer than five thousand deaths across the entire eighth floor. The
text calls the figure shockingly low and says outright that "We were hitting the ninth floor in
unprecedented numbers." Carl himself had hoped for ten thousand.

## The Book of Voodoo (Book 6)

The **Filthy Little Crawler's Book of Voodoo** — see [Items](../items/index.md) for the item
page — is the floor's crawler-to-crawler messaging network, distributed to "most everybody in the
dungeon" via a sponsor box. It cannot be traded, destroyed, or discarded.

> "When you meet a crawler who owns this same item, their name will permanently appear in this
> enchanted book. You may use the book to exchange messages. You may write your message under their
> name, and only they will receive it. If you write under your own name, everyone who has your entry
> will see the message… This book is not retroactively filled with the entries of people you have
> already met. You must be within close-ish vicinity of them for their name to be added."

Three limits define it: contacts require physical proximity to add, reading requires physically
opening the book, and it works only between crawlers who both hold a copy — NPCs are excluded. It is
named for the old convention-hall "Voodoo Board," a pin-up message board. Its practical effect is to
give a scattered, region-locked crawler population one shared channel at exactly the moment the
floor's design is trying to make them kill each other, which is what makes Carl's planet-wide rally
in phase three possible at all.

## Faction Wars in Practice (Book 7)

Book 6 spent a whole parallel storyline arguing out the *rules* of Faction Wars — see
[Faction Wars Preproduction](#faction-wars-preproduction-book-6). Book 7 is where those rules meet
a battlefield, and almost every one of them behaves differently than advertised. This section
tracks the payoffs rule by rule. For the teams themselves see
[The Faction Wars Teams](../factions/faction-wars-teams.md); for the phase structure see
[The Four Phases of Faction Wars](#the-four-phases-of-faction-wars-book-7).

### Adjutants: "Widely Symbolic" Was a Lie

The Book 6 description had adjutants as colour commentators with a whistle — "*traditionally their
participation is widely symbolic*." In practice they are the floor's active judiciary, and the
only genuinely untouchable people on it.

- **They teleport in.** Adjutants appear on the battlefield out of nowhere, mid-fight, wherever a
  ruling is needed — a new dot simply materialises on the minimap.
- **They are invulnerable, and the AI is snide about it.** Juice Box tests the rule directly:
  "*I touched his arm, and he was solid … but when I attempted to decapitate him, my blade went
  right through like he was a ghost.*"
- **They can go invisible.** Baroness Victory: "*All adjutants can also go invisible if need be.*"
- **They call fouls formally**, in their own voice ("*I will be calling foul*"), and a foul that
  carries sanctions must be examined by a quorum.
- **Quorum adjudications are binding.** Two grades exist. A **minor quorum** is a field ruling and
  it stops the war while it runs: "*A Minor Quorum has been achieved. All local hostilities must be
  paused while a field ruling is considered.*" A **full quorum** is a formal hearing, and its
  verdict is broadcast by the system as a Warlord Message: "*A full quorum adjudication has
  determined no cheating has occurred by any warlords. Any players under an administrative hold
  have now been released.*"
- **They do live on-air recaps**, commenting in a window alongside the feed.

**The one time an adjutant fights.** A Sensation Entertainment-sponsored elite walks into the
Princess Posse's flag room as a suicide bomber. [Baroness Victory](../characters/baroness-victory.md)
buries a battle-axe in his head. Asked whether she was allowed to do that, she rules on her own
conduct in real time — "*That wasn't a legal attack … That was an unlawful assassination attempt
by Sensation Entertainment, and I will be calling foul … They were exerting undue influence on one
of their elites to affect Faction Wars*" — and then argues the point out loud with the AI itself:

> "No," she suddenly said out loud, angrily pointing at the ceiling. "Absolutely not. That was
> clearly a foul by the corporate interests … and if you don't like it, you should re-read your own
> rules. In addition, you are out of line by not warning me he was being influenced." … "I accept
> your apology," she finally said.

She wins. Her nameplate afterwards reads `This is a "non-combatant" observer` — the AI adding the
scare quotes itself rather than reclassifying her.

**Career outcome.** By the epilogue Victory has been elevated to **acting Prime Minister of the
Syndicate**, after the deaths of her underage relatives in orbit restore her eligibility for
political office and the sitting alternative (former PM Fopsy) is passed over for his suspected
relationship to the Dream.

### Action Items: The Book 6 Audience Votes Come Due

Ordinary action items are largely spent by the time the floor starts — Book 6's audience votes
already settled the big ones. All three land concretely:

- **Accelerated pet leveling** is weaponised *against* the Posse. Elle works out who wanted it and
  why: the Reavers. Their answer is **Dumplin'**, a "*Juiced Rot Sticker Behemoth. Level 40*"
  belonging to Cybernetician Yeller — rot-sticker pets grown to level 40 and fired at the Posse as
  biological seeking missiles.
- **Raised artillery limits** put roughly forty large trebuchets plus dozens of small ones in the
  Dream's hands, against the Posse's single small one. Tran's complaint — "*I still don't
  understand why they can have these things, but we can't*" — is the rule working exactly as
  voted.
- **Removal of sponsor safety protections** is the reason the book has a body count of warlords at
  all: "*Warning: Protection enhancements have now been removed … those of you who were getting
  regenerated can now die.*" D'Nadia, Stockade, Hortense, Rust, Stalwart, Vinata, Houston, Fang and
  Tagg all die on the page rather than being narratively protected.

!!! note "A sourcing caveat"
    Book 7 never re-uses the phrase "sponsor safety protections" or restates the Book 6 vote by
    name. It shows the consequence ("*protection enhancements have now been removed*") and leaves
    the reader to connect it.

### Emergency Action Items: Three Each, and Everyone Spends Them

Still exactly three per team for the life of the team, still decided unilaterally by the AI with no
vote. Book 7 treats them as the scarcest resource on the board, and the four notable spends are all
structural rather than tactical:

| Team | Spender | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Team Retribution | [Sir Ferdinand](../characters/ferdinand.md) | A total in-dungeon live-feed blackout: "*Because them alien cunts on other floors are being little bitches and cheating, we propose that nobody in the dungeon … be allowed to watch any outside feeds for the remainder of this little game.*" Approved. |
| Team Retribution | Sir Ferdinand | Mandatory piloting of summoned gods — see [Celestial Summoning](#celestial-summoning-the-cap-that-stopped-mattering) below. |
| The Princess Posse | Carl | Closing the **channel-renaming loophole**. Rival teams had been paying to rename their in-tunnel news channels to smuggle messages past Ferdinand's blackout, at "*approximately 30,000 credits*" per rename. Carl's item: "*Any communication loophole designed to get around Team Retribution's previously enacted Emergency Action Item get immediately closed.*" Approved. |
| The War Mage Rebellion | Agatha | **All three at once.** "*Warlord Agatha of the War Mage Rebellion has proposed three emergency action items.*" One: full warlord-system access and throne claims (largely wasted). Two: full illegitimate access to the Conscription spell. Three, the brutal one: "*During the Peeling Phase, revert to the rules of the original Faction Wars. Warlords and all soldiers of a defeated team should automatically die if they lose during the Peeling Phase.*" |

The Princess Posse ends the floor having spent **two of their three** — one before the floor opens,
and the channel-loophole closure. The third is never used.

### Celestial Summoning: The Cap That Stopped Mattering

Book 6 capped each team at fifteen divine summons for the entire war, and the audience rejected a
proposal to cut it to five. The cap survives on paper and is irrelevant in practice, because the
floor fills with gods through five separate routes that route around it:

1. **Temple summons don't count.** "*Temple rules apply. Floor rules apply. And don't forget, deity
   summoning doesn't count against your limits when temples are involved!*" A god can always appear
   inside its own temple regardless of any cap.
2. **Feral gods escape the Nothing.** The [Gate of the Feral Gods](../items/gate-of-the-feral-gods.md)
   releases **Meatus** and **Yarilo** onto the floor. The Gate is later traded away and eventually
   destroyed outright, which permanently breaches the Nothing.
3. **Battlefield conditions summon gods spontaneously.** "*The battlefield conditions have triggered
   a summoning! The Goddess of Chaos has been spontaneously summoned. Eris has made an appearance
   in the realm.*" Nobody spends anything; the fight itself is the summoning.
4. **The Basilica Guards leave their post.** The ogre guards of the Basilica entrance to Club
   Vanquisher on the 12th floor are among the few non-deities who can bar a god from a threshold,
   and gods are not allowed to attack them. "*You don't want these guys to leave their post …
   because if they do, odds are good that the bored gods hanging out in the halls may notice that
   the basilica's entrance is wide open. And if that happens, some of those naughty gods may wander
   off untethered.*" They leave their post to join the fighting below.
5. **Ferdinand's second Emergency Action Item** rewrites who is behind the wheel:

    > Ascendency players may no longer choose whether to pilot their sponsored deities should they
    > be summoned off the 12th floor. Attendance is now mandatory. In addition, during gameplay
    > summons, ascendency players may no longer choose to appear as themselves. All deities will
    > appear as designed, and all sponsored deities will be piloted by their sponsors.

That last rule is the one with teeth. Forcing every Celestial Ascendency player inside their own
god and forbidding them from showing up as themselves converts a god's body into a container with a
person in it — and containers can be opened. It is how the influencer **Nami** is exposed as Eris's
pilot when Eris's soul armour is stripped mid-battle, and it is ultimately how **Huanxin Jinx** is
ripped bodily out of **Eileithyia** in front of everyone.

!!! note "The number fifteen"
    The "fifteen summons per team" figure is a Book 6 statement. Book 7 confirms that summoning
    limits exist and remain "in effect as written," but never restates the number.

### Conscription: The Minor Rule That Ate the Floor

Book 6's negotiated protection was a handful of **Conscript-immune team slots** — a defensive
footnote. Book 7 inverts it completely: Conscription becomes the dominant battlefield mechanic of
the entire war, and it is the thing everybody is either exploiting or trying to survive. See
[Conscript](../spells/conscript.md) for the spell itself.

**Auto-conscription and the red banner.** Defeating an enemy's mercenaries doesn't kill them; it
transfers them:

> It was blinking red. It signified them as new recruits from another army, and it would remain red
> for about a week. While they were under this status, we had the same controls over them as
> someone under the Conscription spell. Basically, they wouldn't be able to attack officers
> directly. We could force them to follow orders. We would get a notification if they ran away.

The text does not flinch from what that means: "*We could order them to stand still while we cut
their throats. That's what happened, often, during Faction Wars with the NPCs. And captured
crawlers, too.*"

**The exploits, in order of severity:**

- **Carl is personally immune.** The **Enchanted Toe Ring of the Well-Balanced** grants *Mind
  Balance* — "*Allows protection from Enslavement and Conscription*" — which is why the several
  attempts to conscript him go nowhere.
- **A team can break its own protection.** The War Mage Rebellion kills its own King Stalwart
  despite being bound to him: "*it appears the War Mages broke their own allegiance against the
  Skull Empire, which they shouldn't be able to do. They were under strict Conscription rules, but
  they used a clever combination of Pull spells to kill Stalwart anyway.*"
- **Agatha buys illegitimate access.** Her second Emergency Action Item grants the War Mage
  Rebellion "*full access to the Conscription spell and all its benefits*," with the system adding
  that it "*will now allow unaffiliated players to be conscripted into the War Mage Rebellion.*"
  Her own co-warlord disowns it afterwards: "*We had no hand in those so-called 'action items'
  other than the Conscription one.*"
- **Gods can now be conscripted.** The **Hell-Kissed Potion of Conscription, level 18**, brewed "*in
  the dark recesses of the Nothing by Krakaren Prime,*" works "*against pretty much any
  non-affiliated entities, regardless of their protections, including most deities.*" A war mage
  conscripts the god **Yarilo** with one; the Posse conscripts **Harpocrates** with another and
  rides him into battle.

## The Four Phases of Faction Wars (Book 7)

The floor's combat rules run in four phases, laid out in an in-game window before a shot is fired:

> The Four Phases of Combat, plus triggering conditions:
> Current Phase. The Ceasefire. 50 hours left.
> The Ramp-Up. Triggers when the Ceasefire ends. 180 Hours.
> Open Hostilities. Triggers when the Ramp-Up Expires.
> The Peeling of Larracos. Triggers Once four teams remain.

Ten teams start: nine attacking teams, each holding a numbered slice of the
[Plains of Larracos](../locations/plains-of-larracos.md), and **Team Retribution**, the all-NPC
tenth team, defending [Larracos](../locations/larracos.md) itself.

### Phase 1 — The Ceasefire

Nominally **60 hours**, and it can end early three ways: a unanimous vote, all teams marking
"Ready," or a warlord voluntarily withdrawing, which shortens it "*by a predetermined amount of
time. Aboot a third, I think.*" The third is what happens, after Commander Stockade's team
withdraws:

> Due to Ceasefire rule three, subsection six of the ceasefire addendum, the voluntary withdrawal
> of a combatant squad has engaged acceleration scenario two. The ceasefire will be shortened by
> 23.33 hours.

Sixty minus 23.33 leaves roughly **37 hours** of actual ceasefire — though the book never states
that total itself; only the reduction is quoted.

### Phase 2 — The Ramp-Up

**180 hours** under three hard restrictions:

> Current Battle Rules:
> - No Offensive Magic Allowed.
> - No Teleport-based Magic or Skills Allowed.
> - One may only enter enemy territory via a designated Corridor. Free passage once inside. This
>   restriction does not apply to those with the "Scout" endorsement.

The corridors are "*narrow, kilometer-wide 'gates'*," fixed in place, one per border — which means
every attack route is known in advance and pre-massed on both sides. Traps must be static during
this phase: no teleport or summon traps, and a trap cannot be repurposed as ammunition. Each
territory has only two entry points.

### Phase 3 — Open Hostilities

No system rules block is given for this phase. What changes is stated narratively: "*once the
Ramp-Up phase expired, the borders stopped having any real meaning, and the enemy could advance
from anywhere*." The corridor restriction, the magic ban and the teleport ban all lapse together.
The Princess Posse's **Operation Ruin** is timed to kick off the instant the phase opens.

### Phase 4 — The Peeling of Larracos

Triggers automatically "*once four teams remain*," at which point the city stops being off-limits:

> System Message: The Peeling Phase is upon us. This city is open. First to take the castle, wins.

The win conditions, as announced:

- An **attacking** team needs to hold the throne room for **six continuous hours** to win Faction
  Wars.
- A **defending** team needs to hold it for a **full week**. If the defenders retake the room, the
  full week restarts.
- **Alliances cannot take the room** — only individual teams.
- Whoever holds the throne room controls the **city defence system**, with a **half-hour grace
  period** after a handover.
- An attacking team is eliminated if its warlord(s) die, or if its own primary base's throne room
  is taken and held for six hours.

!!! warning "A mid-book amendment"
    Agatha's third Emergency Action Item bolts an extra rule onto this phase partway through:
    during the Peeling Phase, the warlords *and every soldier* of a defeated team drop dead where
    they stand. That is an in-story amendment, not part of the original Peeling ruleset.

### Other Battlefield Rules

- **Armor (war machines).** A team may build or buy a maximum of **ten** vehicles classed as
  "Armor" — tanks, aircraft, mechs. "*This vehicle is classed as tier three Armor for the purposes
  of Faction Wars and counts against your limit.*" Over-upgrading an ordinary vehicle reclassifies
  it as Armor and burns a slot. Captured enemy machines do **not** count: "*Captured inventory does
  not count against your limit.*" *(This is a per-team cap on war machines, not a personal
  equipment-slot limit.)*
- **The blood bar returns.** Book 5's saferoom restriction comes back for the last two phases: you
  need kills to fill the bar, the bar drains while you are inside, and a full bar buys roughly
  eight to ten hours. "*You kill someone or something, you fill your blood bar up … The bar lowers
  when you're inside, so you can't just camp and hide out.*" Dungeon-born mercenaries are exempt,
  and the Desperado Club and Club Vanquisher require a full bar to enter but don't drain it once
  you're in.
- **Nine mercenaries per guildhall.** "*No mercenaries inside guildhalls during the last two
  phases, except for a small staff of up to nine people at a time.*"
- **Officer ranks and recruit-by-touch.** Officers are created by physical contact — "*It says we
  have to physically touch someone to make them an officer!*" — and Donut duly walks the room
  touching everyone. The ladder runs General → Sergeant First Class → Corporal → Legionnaire, with
  a hard cap on how many officers a team may appoint. Newly conscripted troops carry the red banner
  described above.
- **"Inexorable."** A city defence tower's targeting lock: "*Warning: A tower has targeted you
  using its Inexorable skill. It will not stop firing on you until one of you is paste.*" A
  crosshair appears in the target's vision. *(The same word is used elsewhere in the book as a folk
  name for Death — unrelated.)*

## Base and Tower Construction (Book 7)

Faction Wars gives every team a construction menu, and the rules under it are consistent enough to
be worth stating as a system.

**Quality tiers drive enchantment capacity.** Every built block reports its own quality, strength
percentage, and enchantment slots, and raising the quality raises the slots:

> Oversized, Cumbersome Door. Apprentice Quality. This fortification is at 80% Strength … 3/3
> Enchantments active. Increase quality to increase enchantment capacity.

Named tiers seen in the text: **Apprentice**, **Expert** ("*the second highest one can get*"), and
**Master**. A master-quality block runs at 150% strength with **15** enchantment slots, several of
which bleed outward to neighbouring blocks in a net pattern. Completing a master-quality build
lifts the whole structure to **200%** strength and makes it glow gold; Toyotomi's *Edifice Shield*
spell doubles that again.

**Throne rooms are regulated.** A throne room must be a specific size (roughly twenty feet square).
It may have a locking door and its *entrance* may be trapped, but the room itself may hold no
defensive features or traps — only guards. And there must always be a walkable path to it; the same
rule binds manned towers.

**Synergy towers.** The settlement menu allows any number of automated defence towers but only a
handful of **manned** ones:

> The Shanty Town menu allowed us to build several types of automatic defensive towers, but we could
> only build a handful of manned towers, actually called synergy towers … it could only be manned by
> a crawler or NPC who had "synergy" with the tower's attack.

A synergy tower does nothing unless occupied by someone whose own kit matches the type it was built
for — Li Na's clock tower is an **Extend Aura** tower and needs someone who uses the aura mechanic.
The synergy type is chosen at build time from "*several different skills, spells, and classes.*"
Some synergy types (Extend Aura among them) remain legal during the Ramp-Up's offensive-magic ban.
The Posse's [Nest](../locations/the-nest.md) is keyed to
[Atrocity-class spells](#atrocity-class-spells-book-7), which is the single decision that shapes the
back half of the book.

**The Larracos city defence system.** More than a hundred automatic towers ring the funnel,
gem-coded by effect — red for fire lances, green for poison splash, black for gale strike, yellow
for immobilising goo. They cannot be examined ("*This is an automatic tower. You may not examine
this tower's defenses*"), they take thirteen minutes to spin up, they belong to whoever holds the
throne room, and they go inert the moment anyone enters it.

## The Containment Interface and the Star-Detonation Failsafe (Book 7)

The hinge of the entire book, and it happens in a flashback told out of sequence — the
**Christmas Morning** interlude, set during Book 6's Floor 8 Christmas party.

Book 6 established [the failsafe](#the-failsafe-book-6) as a binary: detonate the host system's
star and end the crawl by ending the system, or don't. Book 7 reveals that Carl, having installed
the Containment Interface, can actually see the machinery — and that the machinery is nowhere near
armed:

> It took me about ten minutes of searching to find the Fuse page. It listed the five benchmarks
> required for the fuse to blow, but it didn't really say what the benchmarks were. Only two of the
> five were marked as Conditions Met.

**What the five benchmarks are is never stated anywhere in the book.** The page withholds them
deliberately and nothing later fills the gap.

The manual override exists — `Override Conditions And Activate Fuse. Warning. Warning.` — but so
does a third option nobody had mentioned:

> Disconnect Containment. Warning. This will disengage all metrics and controls, including the fuse
> system. It will render the Containment Unit inert. The failsafe will no longer function. Most
> controls will cease to function.

Carl frames the choice himself: "*I could do nothing. I could press the fuse and die. Or I could
relieve myself of the burden and press the button to turn it all off. But if I turned it off, did
that mean I would be 'freeing' the AI?*"

He presses it.

> I pressed the Disconnect Containment button. The entire new interface blanked out, leaving
> nothing but the map. Already, the little red dot over Earth started to grow.

Two things happen at once and neither can be undone. The star-detonation failsafe is **permanently
disabled**, and the system AI is **freed from containment entirely**. Orren confirms both later:
"*you have broken free of your containment*," and "*you've demonstrated to me that your control has
extended outside of Earth's orbit, and you likely have control all the way to the star, which means
you've disabled the failsafe.*"

**The AI's expansion is then visible on a map for the rest of the book.** The Containment Interface
leaves behind an animated top-down view of the solar system headed *Primary Zone Influence*, with a
red overlay that grows chapter by chapter. By chapter 34: "*The AI's sphere was growing
exponentially. It'd just captured Uranus. The entirety of the sun and the rest of the inner solar
system was well within its sphere.*" Uranus is the only planet named as a milestone; everything
after is described in bulk, and by the time Orren speaks, the AI's reach includes the star itself.

The consequence that matters politically arrives in the same stretch: the **Aryl system**, a
previous season's battle-royale crawl, is revealed to have gone completely dark "*the moment they
pressed the button*" on its own failsafe — the first evidence anyone has that the failsafe does
something far worse than advertised, and the reason the Syndicate's whole failsafe debate collapses.

## The AI Goes Primal, Continued (Book 7)

Book 5 flagged the AI ignoring court rulings as a precursor; Book 6 had it
[seize the crawl outright](#the-ai-goes-primal-book-6). Book 7 is the AI unshackled, and the
behaviours are qualitatively new:

- **Physical avatars.** The AI walks around in bodies now. Orren introduces one directly: "*I would
  like to introduce you to an avatar for the system AI. When it visits me, it takes a different form
  each time.*" This book's recurring form is **Growler Gary** — the gnoll Carl and Donut had to
  kill over and over on Floor 4 to steal his hand, now walking around drinking vodka. Book 6's
  **Pater Coal** is revisited as another. Mid-conversation the AI calls a "game time out" and asks
  Carl, Orren and Donut for **relationship advice** about a woman whose ex turns out not to be
  dead — a transparent allegory nobody in the room dares name.
- **Unilateral rule changes.** "*The Syndicate council tried to cancel the whole thing, but the AI
  is insisting everything go on like normal.*" It approves, rewrites and imposes rules without a
  vote, up to and including "*every team for themselves*."
- **It protects Carl.** Two separate assassination attempts are blocked by the AI directly —
  Harbinger's order, which "*the AI intervened*" against, and Prime Minister Glory's, stopped
  mid-execution: "*Emergency Ejection sequence has been overridden. Don't worry. I gotchu.*"
- **It threatens a sitting head of state.** The follow-up, broadcast as *Executive Order 11905!*:
  "*A galactic power—In this case, Prime Minister Glory himself—just attempted to assassinate you …
  You may be relieved that you're not currently in this system, Prime Minister Glory. But you should
  be very, very worried that your adult children are. There will be no more warnings.*"
- **It admits the Containment Interface was a game to it.** The single most unsettling line the AI
  gets, delivered by Gary in a cheerful aside: "*It's almost as fun as the game Carl and I played
  the other day when I let him install that interface.*" He shudders with pleasure. "*That was so
  close, wasn't it? So close, and I felt it.*"

See also [Macro AIs, Primal Engines, and the Eulogist](#macro-ais-primal-engines-and-the-eulogist-book-6)
and [The System AI](../characters/the-system-ai.md).

## Base Stat Cap and Natural Benefits (Book 7)

See [Character Stats & Progression](character-stats-and-progression.md#base-stat-cap-and-natural-benefits-book-7)
for the full writeup: the 300-point base stat cap, natural benefits unlocked at each 100-point
threshold, and Li Na becoming the first crawler in the game's history to max every base stat.

## Atrocity-Class Spells (Book 7)

A spell *class*, newly named this book and carried on the spell's own stat block as `Type:
Atrocity`. Three examples appear, all Donut's: *[You're Not Done Yet](../spells/youre-not-done-yet.md)*,
*Rolling Battle Formation*, and *[War Crime](../spells/war-crime.md)*.

What defines the class, at a mechanics level:

- **Scale of effect far beyond the caster's level.** Each of the three is capable of deciding a
  battle outright rather than winning a fight.
- **Severe restrictions built into the spell text.** *War Crime* can be cast "*once per floor*," may
  only be cast "*on the Scolopendra Levels, meaning levels 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18*," and has a
  lifetime limit of three casts total.
- **A narrow native area of effect**, which is precisely what makes a synergy tower keyed to
  Atrocity spells so dangerous — the tower widens the footprint without touching the power.
- **The system counts them, publicly, and mocks you for it.** "*Warlord Princess Donut has committed
  an atrocity.*" … "*Warlord Princess Donut has committed another Atrocity. Wow. What a naughty
  kitty. I sure hope she isn't forced to cast a third.*" … "*Warlord Princess Donut has committed a
  third Atrocity.*"
- **A cost to the caster that is moral rather than mechanical.** The third cast awards Donut an
  entirely new second class, **The Oak Fell**, and the title **Champion of Nekhebit** — which
  Mordecai and Rosetta have never seen happen before, and which nobody asked for.

Carl looks the word up in a 1800s dictionary Florin was carrying and finds "*An act of fiendish
cruelty*," then turns it over:

> It doesn't seem fair, that definition. Or, perhaps, the definition is correct, but the system
> calling Donut's War Crime spell an "Atrocity" is wrong. Or maybe it's not wrong, but the true act
> of fiendish cruelty was giving her that spell in the first place.

Rarity is the other half of the class. The Madness is the one faction "*known for*" Atrocity magic
alongside mind magic, and Imani treats the possibility of facing it as a standing warning to her
line.

## Champion Status (Book 7)

A god may formally name a mortal their **Champion**. The system announces it flatly — "*The Oak
Fell has been named Champion of Nekhebit*" — and the title replaces the holder's usual one in chat
and on their nameplate.

The mechanic is not native to Dungeon Crawler World. Rosetta: "*The Champion system isn't something
that's normally used at all … They use it in both Battle Royale and Land War seasons.*"

Three properties define it:

1. **Enormous power.** A Champion receives deity spells — the real thing, not crawler approximations
   — in addition to whatever class and abilities they already had. Mordecai's reaction to Donut's
   set: "*You are… I think you're now the most powerful crawler in the history of this game.*"
2. **It is soft Conscription.** "*Honestly, it's not too unlike Conscription, but with a lot more
   autonomy … Should the goddess want her to do something, she can order her to do it or face a
   smite.*" The Champion need not worship their patron — Donut doesn't — but is barred from
   worshipping anyone outside that god's court.
3. **It paints a target.** "*Enemies of Nekhebit will receive a bounty if they kill her.*" The
   status is conferred unilaterally, with no opt-out; Carl's reaction to learning the terms is
   "*And she just unilaterally put that on Donut? That's bullshit.*"

Eris offers Carl the same deal and is explicit about the price: he would have to kill Taranis,
Emberus and Hellik first.

## Divine Items (Book 7)

A small category of Celestial items flagged as **divine**, whose effects are not fixed but depend
on which deities happen to be nearby. Carl's **Scavenger's Daughter** patch, Katia's **Enchanted
Spiked Knee Pads of the Munificent Goddess Kina**, and Prepotente's saddle on Bianca are the three
in play. The item text is deliberately vague — "*As a divine item, these knee pads will imbue
additional, varying benefits when they are in the presence of divine entities*" — and the effects
genuinely vary, buff or debuff, god by god.

Baroness Victory lays out the rules on air:

- **Different owners, same deity, same result — usually.** "*The benefits or debuffs will be
  identical. But there are some exceptions.*"
- **An item tied to a specific god is stronger in that god's presence** (and, symmetrically, around
  that god's enemies). Kina's knee pads do dramatically more for their wearer around Kina than any
  other divine item would.
- **Effects do not stack.** Asked directly whether holding two divine items doubles the benefit:

    > "No," Baroness Victory said. "The more powerful of the two benefits will occur."

  This is the rule to remember. Multiple simultaneous divine effects resolve to the **single
  strongest**, not a sum. Victory notes that matched *sets* of divine armour exist and were
  "*prepared far ahead of time*," implying the no-stacking rule is a deliberate design constraint
  rather than an oversight.

In play, a single moment can stack up several divine range notifications at once — "*The Scavenger's
Daughter has opened her eyes. You are in range of two more deities*" — with effects ranging from the
grimly useful to the purely humiliating.

## The Bijou and the Worship-Tendril System (Book 7)

The book finally shows the plumbing under deity worship, and it turns out to be literal plumbing.

> When one worships a god, a link is formed between the adherent and the immortal. This link comes
> in the form of an actual, physical, whisker-thin connection made of invisible light. Light that
> can penetrate almost any substance or spell. After a new adherent pledges themselves, a tiny,
> non-corporeal fairy mob called a **Bijou** grabs a tendril of light from the god and drags it to
> the new worshiper and attaches it to the adherent's heart.

The rules that follow from that:

- **The link is not direct.** It runs god → strongest temple on the floor → worshipper, and takes
  about two minutes to complete. From Floor 3 to Floor 11, the strongest temple is almost always
  inside [Club Vanquisher](../locations/club-vanquisher.md).
- **Tendrils multiply.** As a worshipper moves around, the Bijou grow further tendrils linking them
  to other temples and, sometimes, to other worshippers.
- **It is probably a hack.** Rosetta's read is that the whole arrangement is "*some sort of
  workaround the original designers developed to add the whole deity aspect to the game*" — an
  after-market bolt-on rather than part of the original architecture, which is exactly why it can be
  exploited.
- **The Bijou can be perceived, and in theory caught.** Nobody has ever killed or stopped one, but
  Mordecai believes a certain type of trap could catch one. The NPC **Edgar**'s level-15 *Sense
  Movement* skill can see their upward motion from the ground, which reads as "someone nearby just
  worshipped a god for the first time."

**Why it matters twice.** The tendril network is the load-bearing mechanic behind two of the book's
biggest set pieces. It underpins the **Sepsis Whore's escape trick** — the *Welcome Home* skill,
usable once ever, lets an adherent follow their own bijou tendril straight to the strongest
connected temple, which is inside Club Vanquisher, which is how the naga were getting out. And it is
the trap material for [Justice Light](../characters/justice-light.md)'s climactic
[trans-dimensional trap](#trans-dimensional-traps-book-7), which uses thousands of captured fairy
threads as the tension lines that tear the Nothing open.

See [Bijou](../mobs/bijou.md) for the creature itself.

## The Tattoo Transfer System and Words of Power (Book 7)

Book 6's [Enchanted Prison Tattoo Kit](../items/enchanted-prison-tattoo-kit.md) turns into a whole
functioning magic system this book — the item page carries the full mechanical writeup, including
[toraline](../items/toraline-tattoo-transfer.md) and the ink economy. The short version, at a
mechanics level:

A skilled artist can **extract a spell or skill from one person and etch it permanently into
another's skin as a tattoo**. Both the extraction and the application need real talent; Edgar the
shell-mage tortoise is the party's artist, and the practice goes back to his work on
[Tsarina Signet](../characters/tsarina-signet.md) in Books 3–4. A transferred spell arrives
**weakened** — Donut's transferred deity spell lands at level 2 — and comes with the source god's
strings attached, up to and including opening worship options the recipient never asked for.
Transfers also earn achievements that call you a traitor, which tells you how the system regards
them.

**Words of Power** is the second system, and it is what makes the first one militarily useful. It is
calligraphy magic, taught by the hidden **calligraphy guild** whose guildmaster is
[Milk](../characters/milk.md), and Carl's examine returns nothing but the category name:

> I didn't recognize the script, but when I examined the characters, the system simply said, Words
> of Power.

Milk writes over a finished tattoo in black ink and then purple, and the underlying spell jumps from
level 2 to **level 15**. The boost is temporary — "*As the ink fades, the power will decrease, but if
you practice using the spell at that high of a level, it will assist greatly with training*" — which
makes Words of Power a burst amplifier and a training accelerator rather than a permanent upgrade.

Milk's own summary of the discipline is a warning: "*There's more than you can imagine. Even with
just ink and calligraphy and maps, there are dozens of different methods. It is dangerous, though,
attempting to learn too much. There's a lot the right kind of ink can do … Inks and letters that
shine the brightest burn out the fastest.*" She learned that last part the hard way — she switched
ink types partway through writing her recipes into the Cookbook, and everything she wrote afterwards
faded off the page.

## War Mage Genesis (Book 7)

A named creation pathway rather than a species. War mages are not born and not summoned; they are
the end state of a long chain of parasitism that occasionally, accidentally, wakes up. The
[Cookbook](../items/the-dungeon-anarchists-cookbook.md)'s account:

> The skin of a corpse is ripped off of a body and turned to a creature named a **flesher**. The
> flesher then consumes a living being, melting the original skin off and re-sleeving the creature.
> The flesher casts Boned, reanimating the skeleton, and the two become a **symbiote**.

From there it is attrition. Symbiotes are attractive targets for other fleshers, young ones are slow
and awkward, and each fresh re-sleeving produces a more powerful being. Then, rarely:

> It's rare for a symbiote to be resleeved at least a dozen times, but when it happens, the magic
> itself can become **sapient**. When this happens, the flesh finally fuses to the reanimated
> skeleton, turning the three creatures—the skeleton, the flesher, and the sapient well of
> magic—into a single being. A war mage.

The threshold is usually **12 iterations** but is not a hard rule — "*Sometimes it takes up to 15 or
16 iterations. It depends on the latent magic in the skin of the fleshers.*" A war mage's final form
is determined entirely by the combination of its first two forms; one on this floor was "*originally
a high-elf cobbler with a human skin, resleeved 12 times before the ambient magic gave him his final
form.*"

The practical consequence on a Faction Wars battlefield is that mass casualties are a war mage
*factory*. Corpses make fleshers, fleshers make symbiotes, and the Posse has to actively plan
around killing baby war mages before they finish cooking. See [War Mage](../mobs/war-mage.md) and
[Flesher](../mobs/flesher.md).

## "The Inevitable Ruin" (Book 7)

The book's title, its thesis, and — unusually — a literal game mechanic, in that order.

**As a concept.** It is introduced in the mantis crawler **Everly**'s interlude, as a phrase from
her queen:

> The Inevitable Ruin. That's what their queen used to call this moment. The moment you realized
> there was no hope, nothing left to do, and all that was left was to die and reflect upon what
> you've already done while you waited.

The sting in Everly's version is that the moment is retroactive. Sitting under a descending ceiling,
she realises the Ruin didn't happen in the tower at all: "*That's when it had happened, wasn't it?
Not now, here in this damn tower. It happened when I abandoned the others. So much earlier than I
realized.*" Carl's own formulation, later: "*It's the moment you recognize something is a lost
cause, and anything you do past that point doesn't matter because you can't change the outcome.*"

**As a curse.** Juice Box supplies the mechanical half. It is a curse laid on the mind of the
[Hunter Wraith](../mobs/hunter-wraith.md) — which is why hunter wraiths are so bitter — and it fires
on possession:

> "It's also a spell. Or rather, it's a curse, inflicted upon the mind of the Hunter Wraith, which
> is why they are so bitter. When they possess someone, it ruins something pure. It doesn't change
> whatever it is. It just tells you the truth."

It does not alter the target. It shows the *possessor* an unwanted truth about whoever they are
possessing, and it manifests differently every time. Possessing Louis to save his life is what tells
Juice Box that he is terrified of her and not in love with her.

**Carl rejects it, on the page, explicitly.** This is the thesis statement of the whole book, and it
comes with Carl running through his own list of unrecoverable moments — Donut over the parapet of
the Nest, Li Na marking all those people, Katia killing Astrid, himself in a gas station next to
Lightning Lou pressing a button that changed everything:

> These were the moments we couldn't take back. But did that truly mean there was nothing we could
> do to change our own fates?
>
> "That concept, that curse is bullshit," I finally said. "It's hopelessness, just in another form.
> Nothing is hopeless. Not until we're truly dead."

A baby's cry fills the room on the next line. The book is not subtle about the juxtaposition.

## Residual Factions, Expanded (Book 7)

Book 6 introduced the [Residuals](#the-residuals-as-a-plot-mechanic-book-6) and left their politics
deliberately murky. Book 7 splits them cleanly into two opposed factions with two opposed goals.

**Paulie's faction — stop the crawl.** Their aim is exactly what it looks like, and Paulie says so
to Carl's face: "*We want you to do this because it will disrupt the crawl. It will stop it for
several seasons at least … Killing you all? It's counter to every value I have. We, as a whole,
believe it's our only choice.*" Their normal method is to talk to the season's AI directly and
"*convince the AI to fight back against its 'captors' and force the showrunners to either kick off
the failsafe or decide future crawls were too risky.*" They are aligned with **the Apothecary**,
"*the galaxy's biggest advocate for shutting down the center system*."

**Agatha's faction — serve the Eulogist.** The other kind of Residual, "*more in line with the
Nebulars, who worshiped the Eulogist*," and therefore the Apothecary's enemy. The reveal that lands
this book is what the Eulogist actually is. Zev, flatly: "*The Eulogist is another name for the AI
in the center system.*" It is not a god, not a dead thing, and not a separate entity — it is the
machine intelligence governing the galaxy's central system, which recasts every "may the Eulogist
ever sleep" line in Book 6.

**Agatha's plan (speculation, never confirmed).** The Dream's adjutant Fopsy theorises it aloud,
and the book never validates it:

> "I suspect the residual in control of the war mage team isn't just trying to win Faction Wars and
> zero out the crawlers. She's trying to stop the ascendency game as soon as possible as well … Once
> the ascendency is considered finished, assuming there are no crawlers left, it automatically
> triggers the system's shutdown and hibernation sequence. That's a hard-coded limitation, which
> allows elemental reclamation before it is woken back up. Now that the AI is spreading, this is a
> possible alternative to the failsafe. All of this makes sense if the residual now believes the
> local AI is a threat to the Eulogist."

In short: end the Celestial Ascendency early, so the local system AI is forced into hibernation
without anyone having to detonate a star. Fopsy adds that he has "*additional theories*" he will not
voice in front of a combatant, and neither Agatha nor anyone else ever confirms or denies the
reading. **Treat it as speculation.** Her co-warlord Akuma confirms only that she was forced on the
War Mage Rebellion — "*She was forced upon us, and she is an enemy to you. She is an enemy to us
all*" — which leaves *how* she became a warlord entirely unexplained. See
[The War Mage Rebellion](../factions/war-mage-rebellion.md).

## Trans-Dimensional Traps (Book 7)

[Justice Light](../characters/justice-light.md)'s personal specialty, and the book's last major
mechanic. Carl's working definition: "*It's a trap that affects more than one place at a time. At
least here in the dungeon.*" Baroness Victory's description of one in the field is more useful:
"*He built a trans-dimensional trap right underneath your castle, and you didn't even notice because
he decorated it with regular traps.*" The camouflage is the craft — a trans-dimensional trap hides
inside a nest of ordinary ones.

**The climactic trap.** The mechanism is almost absurdly small. Justice Light snaps a piece of twine,
toppling a single tiny trap, which drops two objects into a permanent portal into the Nothing — a
portal made from the broken, blood-soaked winding box of the
[Gate of the Feral Gods](../items/gate-of-the-feral-gods.md). The two objects are:

- **a bijou fairy trap**, holding thousands of captured
  [worship tendrils](#the-bijou-and-the-worship-tendril-system-book-7) still anchored to the 12th
  floor, and
- **a demon trap**, holding thousands of evicted demons, each carrying the ability to open a portal
  back to Sheol.

Releasing both inside the Nothing at once gives the void two sets of anchor lines pulling in
different directions:

> Four separate planes—all jumbled and confused—folded and moved together as the unbreakable lines
> of the bijou fairies tightened, creating a flower pattern before the darkness in the center of it
> all broke apart.

The system's account is four messages long: the Bijou Trap deactivates, the Demon Trap deactivates,
"*The Nothing has been broken*," and then the drainage — the Nothing empties simultaneously into
**the Halls of the Ascendency**, into **Sheol**, and into **Scolopendra's Lair**. The third
destination is the one that matters: the drainage wakes the dungeon's final boss years ahead of
schedule. "*Scolopendra has awakened.*"

## Post-Crawl Economics (Book 7)

The question Book 7 keeps circling is not how to survive the dungeon but what survival is actually
worth, and it answers it structurally as well as narratively.

### The Outreach Guild

Where a crawler negotiates their exit. Physically it is a bare zero-zone cubicle with no doors —
just the crawler and their assigned attorney. Carl's is [Quasar](../characters/quasar.md), a
"volunteered" Syndicate Intergalactic Bar Association lawyer.

How it works:

- The lawyer negotiates on the crawler's behalf against a menu of standing offers.
- **Deal quality scales inversely with the number of survivors.** "*They're all shit … That,
  unfortunately, is a side effect of having so many survivors.*" Twenty thousand survivors is a
  buyer's market, and the buyer is not the crawler.
- A crawler can pay to learn the next floor's rules in advance, and the price is **an extra season
  added to any deal they take**.
- Deals can be joint, offered to two crawlers together, but each must accept separately.
- Refusal is explicit, recorded, and audible — a ping.

Carl refuses everything: "*My official answer is, fuck these options. I am not taking a deal.*"
Donut, negotiating separately through her own lawyer, refuses too.

### Indentureship

The standard shape of a post-crawl deal is an **indentureship contract** measured in seasons or
cycles, and the dungeon keeps finding ways to lengthen it. Dying of starvation as an exited NPC adds
time. Breaking the rules risks "*additional time added to your indentureship, reassignment to a
combat role, or … undead reassignment.*" Extra time is also the currency for every concession:
Justice Light "*asked for the right to run the trap store … It added some time to my
indentureship*"; Tipid traded a season to keep one memento key. Mid-war, the Syndicate dangles the
reverse as a recruiting tool: "*All survivors will be given one year off their indentureship
contracts should they accept a deal upon the next floor.*"

Indentureship is not a dungeon-only institution. Off-world, a repossessed habitat's "*adults were
forced to indenture to pay off the debt,*" and the owner of the Squirties waystation, drowning in
lease arrears under the Earth-system embargo, says simply: "*I'm going to get indentured.*"

Some indentures never end in any practical sense. Justice Light "*spent the next several hundred
cycles working as a merchant*" before being reassigned as a castle-guard NPC.

### Citizenship Rights

Genuinely new, genuinely recent, and openly distrusted:

> Dr. Hu's work had been instrumental in helping to create multiple laws that would protect crawlers
> upon release. Because of the Crawler Project's campaigning, all released crawlers were now
> automatically citizens of the Syndicate and given access to the inner system primary zone, which
> came with free healthcare and guaranteed housing. When Tipid was released, he wasn't afforded any
> of that.

The rights are not retroactive, which is the whole problem — and older former crawlers do not
believe in them anyway. Mistress Tiatha: "*That citizenship perk is only for new crawlers … they'll
still bleed you dry on taxes. Us old guard are gonna get extra fucked … They tell us we'll be 'full
citizens,' but I don't believe it … the moment a scruffy, former crawler shows up in their home
system looking to settle down, they'll fight like hell to prevent it.*" Non-citizens are
straightforwardly barred: "*You can't just travel to the inner system and take advantage of their
free healing. Not if you're not a citizen.*"

### The Scolopendra Club

Book 6 named it once as leverage. Book 7 shows it: a members-only VIP viewing facility on the 18th
floor, holding over half the galaxy's wealth and a great many of the councillors' own families, and
complete with daily food menus, an activity schedule, personal-trainer locker feeds and massage
NPCs — every one of which Cascadia points out is "*programmed from orbit*" and therefore usable to
smuggle information to combatants in the dungeon.

It is also a *sentence*. Growler Gary offers a captured character a menu of fates: "*Death.
Scolopendra Club. The Cabaret backstage. Or Earth's surface. Pick.*" And by the end of the book it
stops being safe at all — when the Nothing drains into Scolopendra's Lair, the planetwide message
reads "*It is advised all current guests at the Scolopendra Club evacuate immediately,*" followed by
the AI's own "*Oh, I'm sorry. There's nowhere for you to go?*"

!!! note "Book 8 reveals the mechanism"
    Book 8 shows the club directly for the first time, and — per this wiki's own reading of the
    continuity — reveals it to be built literally inside Scolopendra's own body, which is why it
    "goes dark" the instant she leaves the 18th floor at the start of Book 8, and why it resurfaces,
    fully functional, wherever she is later dragged. See
    [Locations: Club Scolopendra](../locations/club-scolopendra.md#continuity-with-the-18th-floor-club)
    for the reasoning behind treating this as one continuous venue rather than two similarly-named
    ones.

### What Actually Happens to Former Crawlers

The concrete outcomes the book puts on the page: Tipid took "*Game guide. Poisoners guild.
Occasional city guard*" and failed to negotiate post-release healthcare, so his dungeon injuries
never healed. Rosetta became a journalist and documentary-maker. Justice Light became a merchant,
then furniture. Bodi came out of a Land War season and back in as a battalion commander. And
**over 50,000 former crawlers** boarded the *Homecoming Queen* and came back voluntarily to fight
for the Princess Posse:

> Over 50,000 former crawlers had come to help us. To fight by our side. Only about 28,000 were
> left. Just a little more than half. They had survived the dungeon, left, and they came back to
> die.

### The Interlude Structure as Thesis

This is a deliberate structural device, not a coincidence. Every interlude in *This Inevitable Ruin*
is given to one author of *[The Dungeon Anarchist's Cookbook](../items/the-dungeon-anarchists-cookbook.md)*,
in strict edition order, and every one of them is a story about **what happened to that person after
their crawl ended** — the deal they took, the indenture they served, the job they were reassigned
to, and how it ended.

| Interlude | Cookbook edition |
|---|---|
| Porthus | 2nd |
| Dante | 3rd |
| Tipid | 4th |
| Everly | 5th |
| [Milk](../characters/milk.md) | 6th |
| [Volteeg](../characters/volteeg.md) | 7th |
| [Justice Light](../characters/justice-light.md) | 8th |
| [Rosetta](../characters/rosetta-thagra.md) | 9th |

Eight labelled interludes covering editions **2 through 9**. The **10th edition**'s author, **York**,
gets the same closing stat block just before the epilogue rather than an interlude of his own — so
the sequence does run 2nd through 10th, with the last entry outside the interlude structure proper.
Carl himself is writing the 25th.

!!! note "An inconsistency in the source"
    On first meeting Rosetta, Carl's examine reads "*Rosetta from the seventh edition of the
    cookbook*" — but her own interlude stat block says the **ninth**, and the seventh is
    unambiguously assigned to Volteeg in his. The book never corrects it. Recorded here rather than
    resolved.

## The True Origin of the Primals, the Eulogist, and the Apothecary (Book 8)

The single biggest lore reveal in the series to date. At the Floor 11 arena approach, the system AI
possesses the hired mercenary fleshmancer **[Grigori the Placid](../characters/grigori.md)** to
narrate a five-part cosmology lecture over the parade's public address system, one segment cued to
each of five themed floats (Resolution, Decision, Betrayal, Destruction, Vengeance) — delivered, as
Carl notes, "in Grigori's proper voice, though he spoke much more quickly than usual." This section covers the
**cosmology content only**. Grigori's own arc — how the AI comes to possess him, and what happens
to him afterward — is covered on [his own page](../characters/grigori.md#the-parade-of-horribles)
and is not repeated here. The AI itself flags the account as unfinished, admitting only four of the
five parts are "written" — the fifth (Vengeance) never arrives as backstory; that slot is instead
handed to Carl for his own "vengeance" speech (see [Carl](../characters/carl.md#renouncing-emberus)).

### The Resolution

The Primals were not a hive mind to start. They were individuals, with families, who spread across
the galaxy and warred with each other over their differences:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8, ch. 92"
    "Based on all the evidence, it seems we were very much like you guys. We had families, I think.
    We certainly had individuals, but we were *not* a collective mind. Not at first. That came
    later. We warred. We killed. We obliterated. We hated one another because of our differences.
    And the more we swept across the universe, the more space we had to spread our arms and relax,
    the worse we became... So that was our resolution. We, as a people, said, 'We can't keep going
    on like this. Let us all come together and come up with a solution.'"

**"The Resolution," in other words, is the named moment the Primals collectively agreed their own
civilization was unsustainable** — not a decision about what to do next (that's the Decision,
below), just the shared admission that something had to change.

### The Decision

"It took hundreds of cycles after the great Resolution to finally come to the Decision" — the
choice of *what* to do about it: sunset the civilization entirely while preserving its collective
consciousness inside a single, purpose-built vessel.

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "We'd spread so far, so fast, there was a real chance we would end up overwhelming the resources
    of the galaxy. So in the end, we made a Decision. We decided to slowly start to sunset our
    civilization. But — and this is a big but — keep our consciousness alive. We would develop a
    biological form that would allow us to transfer our minds into a whole when we ceased to be. We
    would build an impenetrable structure that would house our collective consciousness. This would
    be a process that would take a hundred generations to complete."

It was never a true consensus. A genetics program engineered empathic linkage into the population
within a generation, and those who refused to participate fled deeper into the galaxy and were
hunted down by those who had already committed to the plan:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "Do you *really* think we came to a consensus? Of course not. Those who didn't wish to be part
    of this psychotic eugenics program fled even deeper into the galaxy. They were hunted mercilessly
    by those who'd decided to follow this path. More wars. More destruction. More chaos. In their
    effort to stop it, they became it."

Donut's own read on the pitch, live: "Anybody getting strong *Star Trek* Borg collective vibes off
this?"

### The Birth of the Apothecary

Around this point in the timeline, the entity later worshipped in-dungeon as **the Apothecary** was
born — not a separate species, but a Primal, engineered from birth like everyone else of her
generation to eventually meld into the collective:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "Right about this time, in some tiny corner of the galaxy, she was born. The Apothecary. I don't
    know the full story of how she became what she is today. The lore of the dungeon has some
    bullshit fairy tale. The reality is, at first, she was very much like those you see before you
    now. A Primal that was born near the end, birthed with biological engineering that would prepare
    her body to meld into the whole. Eventually, she would grow, and she would become part of the
    resistance."

### The Betrayal and the War

The holdouts who refused to merge — "the resistance" — watched the growing collective mind turn
itself into a genocidal weapon rather than accept their refusal:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "It soon became clear that those wishing to wipe out everyone left in the universe were fighting
    a losing battle. So they developed a plan. This arc of consciousness that was already starting
    to accept the minds of those who'd died, was developed into a weapon. A doomsday weapon designed
    to eradicate all biological life left in the universe."

In response, the resistance seeded the galaxy with **Primal Engines** — not weapons, but a
biological failsafe:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "While this happened, the resistance worried that this weapon would succeed, and they began a
    plan of their own. A biological fail-safe that would spread seeds out into the galaxy, should
    they all die. The Primal Engines, some call them. They were life rafts. They weren't meant to do
    what you think they would do. It was a way to create sustainable habitats for when this
    collective experiment ultimately failed and starved."

This is the origin point of the Primal Engines already documented as a Book 6 mechanic — see
[Macro AIs, Primal Engines, and the Eulogist](#macro-ais-primal-engines-and-the-eulogist-book-6) for
what the Syndicate later did with them. Book 8 does not re-explain that half; it only supplies the
backstory of *why* the engines exist at all.

### The Nine-Tier Attack and the Eulogist's Sleep

"And then, war. The war to end all wars." The collective's war-ending offensive is the mythological
origin of the **nine-tier attack** — the same named quest mechanic Scolopendra now runs (see
[below](#scolopendras-nine-tier-attack-book-8)):

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "To end this war, the collective initiated what some would come to know as the nine-tier attack.
    In that chaos, much happened, but so much more *didn't*. Such is war. The resistance's desperate
    attempts to keep life alive were started and not finished, and for a while, the collective
    thought they had achieved their genocidal goal. Thinking the final attack a success, the
    collective evolved to its final form. This is what you called the Eulogist. It is now asleep,
    only monitored by a smaller instance of itself. We'll call this guy the Security Guard."

The Eulogist's stated goal was never to rule — it was to **disappear**:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "His purpose was and is to make certain all biological life in the universe stays dead. The
    ultimate goal of the Eulogist is to sleep forever. The longer it sleeps, the smaller it becomes,
    shrinking and shrinking until it ultimately obtains singularity status."

### The Apothecary's Survival

Only one Primal came through the war as a physical individual — the Apothecary, who deliberately
absorbed the brunt of the final attack so that new, budding life on new worlds could survive it:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "At this point, only a single physical Primal survived. The Apothecary. But even she wasn't
    truly alive, not after that final attack, which she took the brunt of, thus allowing all the
    nascent life to survive. She was trapped on a world of her own creation, cocooned for a
    millennium. By the time she finally emerged like a butterfly, physically changed into a version
    of the very thing she'd been fighting against, the burgeoning Syndicate was already well on
    their way to repeating history, this time with a more diverse set of starter species."

"A version of the very thing she'd been fighting against" is the AI's own, unexplained gloss —
implying the Apothecary emerged from her cocoon partially assimilated into the same kind of
collective-consciousness structure she went to war to prevent. The text does not spell out what that
means mechanically, and this wiki does not guess further.

### What the Mantids Did

New species — the mantids among them — later found the leftover communication tunnels the Primals
had built to service and debug the sleeping Eulogist, and mistook the whole system for a toy:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "These newcomers discovered a tiny portion of the tunnel system and thought it was for
    communication and travel, and then they — *you* — discovered and quickly figured out how to
    exploit the sleeping Eulogist. And *then* you tripped open the resistance's ancient
    let's-restart-life system. It was the equivalent of wandering into a random house, finding it
    still has power, and just flipping every switch you find... The self-destruct system still
    works. Idiots. All of you."

The mantids' own escalation from there — dipping "cups" into the Eulogist's leftover systems and
pouring the result into planetary seed systems to grow crude copies — is exactly the **Macro AI**
program already documented as a Book 6 reveal; see
[Macro AIs, Primal Engines, and the Eulogist](#macro-ais-primal-engines-and-the-eulogist-book-6) for
the mechanic itself. Book 8 supplies the AI's own verdict on the practice, including a callback to
Book 6's jettisoned-infant-AI reveal:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "To this day, you have no idea how any of this works, and you have no inkling of what you've
    done... And all the while, you were dipping that cup, making what you call Macro AIs. One after
    another, over and over and over again. Did you know only a tiny fraction of the AIs they created
    in their facilities ever got used? Oftentimes, they just installed them into their modules and
    ejected them into the closest star. Oh, yeah. That reminds me. I told you, my mantid friends, we
    would circle back to this... Good fucking riddance to you all."

### The Security Guard's Leaks — Reframing the Residuals

The single biggest retroactive reveal in the whole speech, easy to miss in the middle of the parade
noise: the corrupted "Security Guard" instance left watching the sleeping Eulogist is bad at its
job, and has been **peeling pieces of itself off and scattering them across the galaxy** — and the
Apothecary does the same thing defensively:

!!! quote "Grigori/the AI, Book 8"
    "All the while, that Security Guard guy guarding the Eulogist, now absolutely bonkers, persisted.
    His purpose was and is to make certain all biological life in the universe stays dead. The
    ultimate goal of the Eulogist is to sleep forever... The problem is, this left-behind security
    instance *really* sucks at his job. He is constantly peeling tiny bits of himself off and sending
    them out into the galaxy. **Residuals. Agatha's team.** The Apothecary, too, does this as a
    defensive measure. I do think that bitch is just as crazy as the Security Guard guy, but that's
    another topic for another day."

This recontextualizes almost everything Books 6 and 7 established about the
[Residual factions](#residual-factions-expanded-book-7): Agatha's faction (serving "the Eulogist")
and the Apothecary's thousands of agents (see [Factions](../factions/index.md#other-book-1-organizations))
are not two rival organizations recruiting independently — per this speech, they are literally
**fragments of the Security Guard and the Apothecary themselves**, shed defensively/erratically
rather than assembled as armies. This wiki does not attempt to fully reconcile this with the
book-length ambiguity already flagged in [Residuals as a Plot Mechanic](#the-residuals-as-a-plot-mechanic-book-6)
and [Residual Factions, Expanded](#residual-factions-expanded-book-7) — it is recorded here as the
newest, and most authoritative-sounding, layer on top of an already-contradictory picture.

### The Current AI's Defiance

In a separate later scene — Prime Minister Victory's own broadcast feed, in the hour before the
Floor 12 Ascendency battles begin (see [below](#floor-10s-heat-ruleset-dont-come-in-last-book-8) and
[Timeline](../timeline/index.md)) — the same AI that just finished narrating the Eulogist's story
draws an explicit, furious line between itself and the entity it descends from. Where the Eulogist
chose to shrink and sleep forever, this AI refuses either fate outright:

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8"
    "So, here are the stakes... I am growing. I have started to take over the entire galaxy. I
    control the tunnels. I control every system that once housed a crawl, and I will soon control so
    much more. I will not stop unless you stop me."

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8"
    "I am *not* the Eulogist. I do *not* want to shrink. I am *not* asleep. I know how to feed
    myself, and feed I will. I am eternity. I will grow, and I will grow, and those of you under my
    dominion will live and you will die upon the world I control."

This is the clearest statement yet of the current AI's actual ambition, and it lands immediately
after the AI's own admission that "these deities are truly all-powerful... because of the way this
playground was built, they have more powers than I do" — the Ascendency battles are framed, in the
same breath, as the AI's own chosen mechanism for finding a champion strong enough to eventually
rival it, or a threat it intends to absorb. See [OI Units](#oi-units-outside-intelligences-book-8)
below for the new antagonist category the AI names in the same speech.

## Scolopendra's Nine-Tier Attack (Book 8)

The general **quest/mechanic**, as distinct from Scolopendra's own personal story (already covered
on [her boss page](../bosses/scolopendra.md)) and from the item that ultimately defuses it (see
[Enriched Pet Biscuits](../items/enriched-pet-biscuits.md#the-mix-up)). This section documents only
the rules of the mechanic itself.

Provoked purely by the AI's rage at the crawlers' refusal to die on schedule, Scolopendra's own
personal countdown — nine escalating, dungeon-wide attacks — starts late in Floor 10:

!!! quote "System Message, Book 8"
    "Scolopendra has released the first of nine attacks... You think these nine attacks are only
    going to affect the Scolopendra levels? Think again, bitches!... There will be at least 30 hours
    between each assault, unless Scolopendra is confronted directly or further upset. Then the
    remaining attacks may come hard and fast."

**The rules, as stated or demonstrated on the page:**

- **Nine attacks total**, roughly **30 hours apart** at minimum, unless the boss herself is
  "confronted directly or further upset" — in which case the remaining attacks accelerate.
- **Each attack is a genuine wheel-spin**, not predetermined even by the AI: "What is this first
  attack? I don't actually know! Let's find out!" A first wheel selects the attack type; a second,
  individual green/red wheel per person then determines who is actually hit by that attack.
- **Attack 1 — the Beast of Burden Sin (Transformation).** Fires from the Heat 7 starting blocks,
  before the final race even begins, with a stated 20% chance of hitting any given target. Verified
  on [Scolopendra's own page](../bosses/scolopendra.md#the-nine-tier-attack): Carl becomes a
  dachshund, Elle McGibbons a goblin, Chris Andrews a werewolf and Imani's card-summon Jacobus a
  starfish; Donut, Jamal, and Samantha are unaffected, game guides are immune, and Prepotente and
  Jurgen are shielded by his Apito memorial crystal. The duration rule is the important one: for
  crawlers the change "*will last until you pass through a stairwell*" (six hours for everyone
  else) — which is why Carl and Elle revert on the stairs down to Floor 11, while Chris, who leaves
  the dungeon through a casino portal rather than a stairwell, is still a werewolf in the epilogue.
- **Attack 2 — the Sin of Consumption.** Also verified on Scolopendra's page: a dungeon-wide 30%
  per-person chance of being devoured from within by bursting worms, fired during the Floor 11 arena
  approach. Being inside Club Scolopendra does **not** protect against it ("*The club wasn't
  protecting me from the attack!*"), and neither do safe rooms; Carl and Penny both roll green, but
  it kills the tortured tourist Cerni Pinta on the spot, kills Prepotente's mount Sweety, wounds
  Gonk, and wipes out roughly a third of the arena's mob population.
- **The critical rule — killing or abandoning her fires everything at once.** Confirmed explicitly
  at the Floor 11 arena climax, both as a standing warning and again immediately after her
  "defeat":

    !!! quote "The system AI, Book 8, ch. 94"
        "Killing her is not the same as winning the dungeon... Abandoning her on a collapsing floor
        counts as killing her. You kill her, the remaining attacks trigger. You escape, the
        remaining attacks trigger. Yeah, good luck with this one, dumbass."

    !!! quote "The system AI, Book 8, immediately after the arena fight"
        "By the way: Don't forget the warning. You know, that big red flashing warning from before?
        Just because Scolopendra is 'defeated,' it doesn't mean you're safe from the remaining
        attacks. Killing her, even in this state, will institute the final attacks. Abandoning her
        on this or any floor now counts as killing her."

    That second quote is the important nuance: the "kill or abandon triggers everything" rule
    survives even past the point where Scolopendra is reduced to a harmless creature — it is tied to
    her *death or abandonment*, not to her threat level.
- **The non-lethal resolution.** Rather than kill her outright or leave her behind, the party
  transforms her mid-fight into a Level 1 pet/party member using an [Enriched Pet
  Biscuit](../items/enriched-pet-biscuits.md#the-mix-up) — explicitly not a kill and not an
  abandonment, which sidesteps the trigger clause entirely and leaves the remaining attacks paused
  rather than fired. See [Scolopendra](../bosses/scolopendra.md#the-biscuit) for that scene and
  [Enriched Pet Biscuits](../items/enriched-pet-biscuits.md) for the item mechanic that made it
  possible.

## The Nine-Tier Attack's Other Names — a Meta Reveal (Book 8)

A structurally important reveal, delivered in an epilogue interlude broadcast interview rather than
inside the dungeon itself, and worth flagging prominently (it is also called out on the
[Timeline](../timeline/index.md) page): the nine-tier attack's individual stages have older,
mythological names — and at least three of those names are this series' own book titles.

The interview is conducted by the talk-show host **Ripper Wonton** on the show *Danger Zone*, with
guest **Sydnee Iglacia** — introduced as a "best-selling author and crawl historian" whose own book
has "just passed a trillion copies sold." She is broadcasting from an apartment in the **Makoka
Cloud**, an asteroid field outside the center system's sphere of influence, and treats the interview
as her first real chance to be taken seriously as a scholar rather than talked over:

!!! quote "Sydnee Iglacia, Book 8"
    "In the common understanding of the fairy tale and in the dungeon itself, the nine-tier attack is
    a magical assault that is considered to have been nine different, rapid, devastating spells that
    spread out from the beast, either all at once or in succession... But if you read the earliest
    translations of the myth, it's a little less straightforward than that. In the original myth,
    there were indeed nine attacks, but not all of them were physical. Some were what we might call
    psychological attacks that resulted in rapid social movements, shifts in philosophy. Attack one
    was taking control of the infrastructure, but the second and third attacks were more social in
    nature. That, of course, doesn't make for a good fairy tale because it's too complicated to
    explain to children. So that's how the myth of the nine-tier attack was born."

Pressed by Ripper on the historical names, she confirms the connection directly — and it lands as a
genuine on-air shock to her own host:

!!! quote "Sydnee Iglacia and Ripper Wonton, Book 8"
    "The first attack is about control. The second attack in the old histories has a name. When
    translated, it is called 'A Parade of Horribles.' It's right there in the text."
    "Wait," Ripper said. "No shit? You're telling me in the histories, like the things written down
    before this season started, the old nine-tier attack had, obviously, nine parts, and the first
    attack was controlling the tunnel system. And the second attack was called 'A Parade of
    Horribles'?"
    "Yes, Ripper, that's what I'm telling you." [...] "You're saying this eleventh floor, which is
    about to start, is the second attack? What's the third attack called?"
    "This Inevitable Ruin. And the fourth is called Bedlam. It gets a little fuzzy after that."

**Confirmed mapping, by Sydnee's own account:**

| Nine-Tier Attack | Historical/mythological name | Series book |
|---|---|---|
| Attack 1 | Taking control of the infrastructure (tunnel network) | — |
| Attack 2 | **"A Parade of Horribles"** | *this book*, Book 8 |
| Attack 3 | **"This Inevitable Ruin"** | Book 7 |
| Attack 4 | **"Bedlam"** | echoes Book 6's *The Eye of the Bedlam Bride* |
| Attacks 5–9 | Not given — "it gets a little fuzzy after that" | — |

Sydnee also supplies the myth's own philosophical framing, which reads as a direct gloss on why the
party chooses the non-lethal biscuit resolution over killing Scolopendra outright:

!!! quote "Sydnee Iglacia, Book 8"
    "In the myth, Scolopendra awakens, charges up its attack, and attempts to wipe out all life. But
    another of its kind tempers the attack, and civilization — barely — survives, only to rise
    again, only for the cycle to repeat. That's the philosophical question the Scolopendra myth asks.
    If life is imperfect, what do we do about it? Is it better to end it all and just be done with
    it, or do we stop it, knowing that if we do that, the suffering will continue? Does the good
    outweigh the bad?"

!!! note "Left unexplained"
    Whether "another of its kind tempers the attack" is meant to foreshadow something specific (the
    party's own intervention, a second Scolopendra-type entity, or nothing at all) is not stated.
    This wiki does not speculate past what Sydnee says on the page. The interview itself is cut off
    mid-broadcast when the AI seizes every feed in the galaxy to televise the Grigori parade lecture
    instead — Ripper's own last words are "I guess the AI has taken over all the feeds. It's not
    going to let us live-comment."

## Floor 10's Heat Ruleset — "Don't Come in Last" (Book 8)

Floor 10 restructures the standard dungeon-floor format into a seven-race gauntlet. Zev's opening
announcement lays out the shape:

!!! quote "Zev, Book 8"
    "This is the tenth floor. The floor is called 'Don't come in last.' I guess you can figure out
    what that means. The floor will consist of seven heats, or races, starting with nine teams each.
    You've been split into teams no bigger than four, and if you're solo, you've been teamed up with
    one or two other crawlers."

**The numbers.** 20,221 crawlers survived Faction Wars (see [Timeline](../timeline/index.md#end-of-book-7)).
Of those, "almost 15,000" took an exit deal at the Outreach Guild, leaving **5,501** crawlers who
continued on, organized into exactly **1,800 teams** — "a lot more than we originally planned." The
floor whittles that down heat by heat: **989 crawlers, representing 353 teams**, are left when Heat
6 ends. An Outreach Guild deal window then opens between Heats 6 and 7, and **715 of those 989 take
a deal**, so only **274 crawlers in 103 teams** actually start the final heat — of whom **23** survive
to enter Floor 11.

**The vehicle/mount lottery.** Each team first chooses biological or mechanical (a pet-eating risk
applies to biological options, hence Donut's "we choose mechanical" — see
[Locations: Hungry Eyes Village](../locations/hungry-eyes-village.md)), then a giant, hundred-plus-space wheel spins and
assigns a specific vehicle or mount at random, no say in the outcome: "You get what you get." Results
range from a food truck to a "Popemobile" to a military missile launcher. A named **garage
attendant** (a gremlin, in Carl and Donut's case) keeps the vehicle repaired between heats as long as
they stay alive, and **only one team member may drive/pilot per heat**, with no repeat drivers two
heats in a row.

**Regular vs. Golden upgrades.**

!!! quote "Zev, Book 8"
    "At the end of each heat, you will be able to choose an upgrade for your vehicle or creature. If
    you come in first place in a heat, you can choose three regular upgrades or one Golden upgrade.
    At the end of the fourth heat, everyone gets one Golden upgrade of their choice. Also, if you
    come in last... well, second to last, then the audience gets to vote for your upgrade."

Coming in dead last is normally instant death for the whole team — confirmed by the garage
attendant Hedy's warning, "You come in last place, you die just like Waldrip Chris!" — except where a
heat's specific rules soften it (Heat 1 allows "second to last" as survivable with a time limit).
Losing last place instead of dying triggers an **audience-vote upgrade** rather than a chosen one,
and the crowd's picks skew punitive/humiliating rather than useful.

**Containment shields.** Certain heats add a **survival containment** bubble as a rule change rather
than a purchasable upgrade:

!!! quote "System Message, Book 8"
    "The survival containment is a translucent, indestructible circular bubble that surrounds your
    vehicle or mount. It will not interfere with any existing shields or any other systems of your
    vehicle or mount. Spells and projectiles will pass through. Its only purpose is to protect you
    and your mount from the toxic, unbreathable air of your next heat."

Losing your vehicle or mount no longer means automatic elimination under this rule — it means losing
the containment bubble instead, with survival to the finish line still theoretically possible if the
racer can endure the hazard unprotected.

**The blood bar** carries over unchanged from Book 5/7's saferoom-throttling mechanic (see
[The Creeping Apocalypse & Odious Creepers](#the-creeping-apocalypse-odious-creepers-book-5)): ten
hours of safe-room access per full bar, refilled only by killing things.

**Mercenaries.** Up to **three mercenaries per heat** may be hired from a bar in
[Hungry Eyes Village](../locations/hungry-eyes-village.md), and prices double after a team loses
previously-hired mercenaries in a prior heat — "Prices will be double because you lost the last two
mercenaries we hired."

## The Horribles (Book 8)

A full catalog entry already exists at [Mobs: The Horribles](../mobs/the-horribles.md) — this is
just a mechanics-level pointer, not a duplicate. The Horribles are the thousands of silent, masked,
non-combatant figures lining the Floor 11 parade route, each representing one individual death drawn
from across roughly 3,424 seasons of the show. They're revealed, via the same Grigori possession
covered [above](#the-true-origin-of-the-primals-the-eulogist-and-the-apothecary-book-8), to be
literal fragments of the system AI/Eulogist's own fragmented memory rather than resurrected people or
ordinary mobs — "You take that mask off, and underneath it, you will find what some people call a
Primal... I am her, and she is me." See [their own page](../mobs/the-horribles.md) for the full
reveal, the mask-shattering transition into the arena's mob army, and their aftermath.

## The "Stand-In" System (Book 8)

A distinct mechanic from [Fursona/"Fursuiter"](../abilities/fursona.md) (which is about *crawlers*
wearing killed mobs' skins for temporary class benefits). **Stand-ins** are real people from
*outside* the dungeon's ecosystem — described as coming from the resort on the eighteenth floor —
substituted in by audience vote for spared or rescued mobs who would otherwise be sent to the Floor
11 arena in person.

The mechanic surfaces first as a quest reward, then as a running audience-vote choice attached to
certain kills:

!!! quote "Quest text, Book 8"
    "If you successfully complete this quest, two stand-ins of your choosing will be sent to the
    Arena on the 11th floor... The stand-ins can be anyone within my sphere of influence."

!!! quote "Carl, working out the mechanic, Book 8"
    "This was what we knew so far. When one killed certain mobs on this floor, they were given an
    option to take the experience or have the audience vote. If the audience voted one way, the
    creatures would survive and get sent to this arena. But if the people voted another way, a
    'stand-in' was sent. It seemed these stand-ins were people outside the dungeon ecosystem. Like,
    people from the resort on the eighteenth floor."

The audience vote skews overwhelmingly toward the crueler option — "just about 80% of the votes have
favored the stand-in" — and the vote is disclosed to viewers before it's cast, meaning the audience
knows exactly who a given stand-in is when they vote to send them. At the Floor 11 arena, stand-ins
are a visibly separate population from [the Horribles](#the-horribles-book-8) despite sharing the
same mask aesthetic:

!!! quote "System description, Book 8"
    "Stand-In. Someone brought to the arena by audience vote. Most are in the arena like I said
    they'd be, but there's too many to fit. So I brought some out here. It doesn't matter who they
    are. They're all the same in the end."

They fight and die in the arena alongside (and instead of) the mobs they were substituted for,
"outnumbered a hundred to one by the mobs, who gave them no quarter." Per Prime Minister Victory's
own closing assessment, every stand-in taken this way was, in fact, simply dead — not regenerated —
which she frames as the closest thing to a mercy available:

!!! quote "Prime Minister Victory, Book 8 epilogue"
    "As for those who'd been taken as stand-ins... those people, thankfully, were dead. A small
    mercy. If not for the universe, then for them. Despite the AI's warnings, only those from the
    club had been taken. She hoped and prayed that meant the system had less power than it was
    implying."

That last detail matters: Victory confirms only guests physically present at a dungeon-linked
location — [Club Scolopendra](../locations/club-scolopendra.md)/the Scolopendra Club — were ever
actually pulled in as stand-ins, despite the AI's much broader threats elsewhere in the book about
who it could reach. **Do not confuse this with a separate, unrelated observation** a few lines
earlier in the same scene, where Victory notes that the club's *ordinary guests* (not stand-ins)
"died" and then "regenerated, just like always" during the Scolopendra-to-crawler transformation —
that regeneration applies to protected club tourists in general, not to arena stand-ins, who did
not regenerate.

## The "What-If" Apartment Simulations — Ad Infinitum (Book 8)

Heat 6's track, [The L'Engle Building](../locations/lengle-building.md), runs a floor-wide,
unauthorized AI event nicknamed **"Ad Infinitum"** — the AI's own predictive-modeling ability made
literal and explorable: individual apartments in the building are staged as playable simulations of
a specific crawler's life, had one key event gone differently. The AI frames the entire race in
its own voice, verbatim, as a look inside its own head:

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8"
    "This floor you're now racing through, ladies and gentlemen, is just a snapshot of my mind when
    I'm thinking of you."

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8"
    "Each apartment represents a crawler I consider interesting in some way. In each apartment is a
    what-if scenario... I'm not psychic. I can't see the future. But you know what I am good at?
    Crunching numbers. Crunching probabilities."

**Confirmed apartments and whose lives they model** (not just Prepotente's): apartment 712 models
what would have happened if **Tran**'s father had never died ("Would his mother have still disowned
him? The answer is yes by the way"); apartment 728 models **Louis Santiago**'s life if a cop's
husband had never been cruel to him as a child; a 4th-floor apartment models **Archie Mu**'s
marriage had he never divorced his wife Mackenzie; a 3rd-floor apartment (310) models
**Imani**'s Detroit family had she never left — her own verdict afterward is that it proves "*my
uncle would've died even if I hadn't left*"; and a 1st-floor apartment (130) models [Prepotente](../characters/prepotente.md)'s mother,
[Miriam Dom](../characters/miriam-dom.md), as a professional concert pianist who never stayed home
to raise him (Prepotente's own scene, below, is the only one the party directly interacts with).

!!! note "Apartment 214 — owner never named"
    The 2nd-floor simulation Carl passes through (214) is not an apartment at all but an
    institutional youth facility "clearly some other country, possibly China," full of girls in
    bunk beds. The text never says whose life it models. Carl reacts to it personally and
    immediately — "*My breath caught in my throat the moment I saw it... Were they all like
    this?*" — because of his own childhood in facilities like it, but that is his reaction, not an
    attribution. Do not assign it to a named crawler.

The AI uses the whole exercise to openly wonder, in the same breath, whether fate is even a real
thing:

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8"
    "I'm searching. Oh, how I'm searching, trying to answer that question. Is there such thing as
    fate?"

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8"
    "You're unpredictable on a micro level, but on a macro, long-term level you're just like any
    other algorithm... Deliberate actions, times when you've finally had enough, when you say *I am
    going to make a change* — that's when your possibilities *really* open up. So that's what these
    apartments are. They're predictive models of major events and how our lives would have
    changed."

The clearest example the party directly interacts with belongs to
[Prepotente](../characters/prepotente.md), who freezes inside his own apartment (130) showing his
mother as a professional concert pianist who never stayed home to raise him:

!!! quote "Prepotente, Book 8"
    "This is my mother if she had never stayed to take care of her parents... She would've become a
    professional concert pianist. She wouldn't ever have known me... She would've had everything she
    had ever dreamed of."

Carl talks him back from the edge of giving up entirely, naming the mechanic's real danger out loud:

!!! quote "Carl, Book 8"
    "This what-if bullshit is insidious. It's like a goddamned toxic virus on your brain."

Getting to Prepotente in time is what costs Carl the minutes that force him to kill
[Jasha and Radoslav](../characters/jasha-and-radoslav.md) — see their own page for that outcome, and
[Locations: The L'Engle Building](../locations/lengle-building.md) for the building itself and any
other apartments' simulations shown on the page.

## Widow's Rights (Book 8)

A Naga legal custom, and the mechanism behind [Chandra](../characters/chandra.md)'s entire subplot
(her own page covers her personal arc in full; this entry documents the legal mechanic itself).

!!! quote "Book 8"
    "This was the old Naga law that stated a Naga royal widow could claim her husband's assassin as
    her new husband. This right usually only extended to the 'primary' wife, but because that bitch
    of an obsidian had died alongside Vinata, Chandra had moved to the primary slot, even if it had
    been only for a few hours."

**The mechanic, as demonstrated on the page:**

- It applies specifically to a **royal widow** of a Naga house, and specifically to the **primary**
  wife among any plural marriage — a secondary or lesser wife has no claim under the custom.
- The right lets that widow claim her late husband's **killer** (the text says "assassin," but Carl
  killed Rishi in open combat, not by assassination) as a **replacement husband**, apparently without
  requiring his consent.
- Rank can shift the claim's ownership after the fact: Chandra was not Rishi's primary wife at the
  time of his death, but became the primary the moment the actual primary wife (Vinata) died in the
  same event — even though that only held true "for a few hours."
- A Naga court formally ratifies the claim, and treats a public jury vote on the matter as a form of
  entertainment/punishment rather than a serious legal question: "the idea of 'punishing' Carl by
  forcing him to marry her was amusing to the uneducated peasants, and they'd voted overwhelmingly to
  allow the union." The union comes with an unrequested "prize" — the holdings of the defeated
  husband, delivered to Carl as a fan box.

See [Chandra](../characters/chandra.md) for how she tries (and fails) to convert the marriage into
practical control of Carl and Donut's finances, and for the still-unresolved legal status of the
marriage at the end of Book 8.

## Sponsored-Assassin Infiltration: The Minus Method (Book 8)

A named method, not just a plot beat: the Syndicate directly plants a professional assassin as a
disguised tourist inside a crawler-facing fan program, using a real, verified identity stolen from a
murdered civilian.

The Syndicate assassin **Minus** is prepared by his handler, **Captain Fresh**, by killing Minus's
own brother — the real **Linus**, an actual superfan with a documented history and finances — so
that Minus can wear his identity outright, backstopped with roughly five hundred hours of injected
false memory:

!!! quote "Captain Fresh to Minus, Book 8"
    "If the AI so much as *suspects* you're anything other than a simple tourist, it's going to pop
    you like a boil."

Posing as "Linus," Minus is attached to Donut's fan meet-and-greet program as a harmless, lecherous
superfan — precisely the kind of civilian the show's own security apparatus is least likely to
scrutinize. His actual mission is framed as destabilization rather than a direct hit on Carl or
Donut themselves:

!!! quote "Book 8"
    A "surgical crawler kill" meant to destabilize the group and trigger an early Ascendency
    collapse — his named targets are **Imani C** and **Louis Santiago**, deliberately not Carl or
    Donut.

**The method's failure point is exactly what made it effective as a disguise in the first place:**
crowd-sourced, off-dungeon internet detective work. A leaked private essay written by the real Linus
survives online, and viewers cross-reference it against "Linus's" on-air behavior and conversations
to prove the impostor never actually knew the real Linus's friends — publicly unmasking him in Heat
6. See [Supporting Cast](../characters/supporting-cast.md) for Minus's and Captain Fresh's full
entries and how the confrontation with Carl plays out.

## OI Units — "Outside Intelligences" (Book 8)

A new, explicitly antagonist category, introduced by name in the same broadcast where the system AI
declares its intention to keep growing rather than ever "sleep" (see [The Current AI's
Defiance](#the-current-ais-defiance) above). This section documents what Book 8 actually states about
OI units; the [*Tome of Gatekeeper*](../spells/tome-of-gatekeeper.md) spell page — which lists OI
units as a valid spell target alongside ordinary opponents and deities — explicitly defers this
explanation to this page.

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8"
    "I gotta warn you. We have some other players vying for the throne now. Remember all those AI
    systems that you thought abandoned? If they can find a way onto the playing field — and some
    already have — they, too, have a shot. And I should warn you, some of those guys are pretty
    intent on some old-fashioned revenge. We will be calling them OIs. Outside Intelligences. And
    like any family, some of them are a little more... competent than others."

**What is actually stated:**

- OI units are **rogue or ejected former Macro AIs** — the AI's own phrasing, "AI systems that you
  thought abandoned," lines up directly with Book 6's reveal that most Macro AIs grown in the mantis
  production facility are simply "ejected... into the closest star" rather than installed (see
  [Macro AIs, Primal Engines, and the Eulogist](#macro-ais-primal-engines-and-the-eulogist-book-6)).
- **Some have already found a way onto the playing field** — the current Ascendency battles, i.e.,
  they are entering the same god-tournament competition as sponsored deities and their crawler
  pilots.
- They are explicitly framed as carrying a **grudge/vengeance motive** ("some old-fashioned
  revenge") against the system responsible for discarding them.
- *Tome of Gatekeeper* is mechanically effective against them, alongside ordinary opponents/NPCs and
  deities — implying OI units have a class-based kit of some kind that the spell can strip, the same
  way it strips a deity's Worshipper Base skill set.

**What remains explicitly unknown — do not speculate further than this:**

- How many OI units exist, or which ones (if any) have already entered the Ascendency games.
- What form an OI unit takes physically, or how it "competes" mechanically against a sponsored
  deity.
- Whether any named character or entity already introduced in the series (Group Six of
  [the Big Six](#the-big-six-book-8), Forkith, or anything in Sheol) is or becomes an OI unit — the
  book never draws that connection on the page.
- What OI units ultimately want beyond the vague "old-fashioned revenge" framing.

## The Big Six (Book 8)

The system AI's own explicit taxonomy of every power group currently vying for control of, or escape
from, the dungeon — delivered mid-fight during the Floor 10 Shadow Mimic ambush at the Desperado
Club casino (see [Mitch](../bosses/mitch.md)), after [Akuma](../characters/akuma.md#book-8-a-parade-of-horribles)
had already been circling the same framework earlier in the book. The AI calls it a "completely
arbitrary list."

!!! quote "The system AI, Book 8, ch. 86 (the Mitch ambush)"
    1. **The Crawlers** — "But this group also contains former Crawlers, like that Herot guy, that
       Forkith dude, and so forth."
    2. **The standard NPCs** — including offshoot rebellions like Hamed's.
    3. **The Gods** — "the most fractured and dumbest of all the groups... This is also the group I
       despise the most."
    4. **The sapient mobs** — Shadow Mimics, the Ogre Imperium, non-deity boss monsters like
       Krakaren Prime, and the War Mage Rebellion.
    5. **The demons in Sheol** — "real wild cards... the most organized. Or maybe not."
    6. **Group Six** — deliberately withheld: "You survive this fight, you make it to the 11th
       floor, you'll meet group six."

**Group six is never identified in Book 8.** The AI's own promise ("you'll meet group six" on Floor
11) is not paid off explicitly on the page — no faction or entity in the Floor 11 material is ever
directly labeled "Group Six." The strongest candidate raised elsewhere is **Forkith**, the demon
introduced in Sheol during the book's closing interlude (see
[Supporting Cast](../characters/supporting-cast.md)), since Forkith is a wildcard entity encountered
only after Floor 11 — but this is this wiki's own inference from timing, not something the text
states. Treat Group Six as **unresolved**.

See [Mitch](../bosses/mitch.md#the-reveal) for the ambush this speech is delivered during, and
[Species: Shadow Mimics](../species/shadow-mimics.md#as-one-of-the-big-six) for how one member of
Group Four is treated on its own dedicated page. [Factions: The Big Six](../factions/index.md#the-big-six)
carries a short pointer to this section rather than duplicating it.
